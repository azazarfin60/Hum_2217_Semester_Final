# L03.1: Consideration Definition, Types, and Rules

> [!info] ⚡ Exam Snapshot
> - **Section & Priority:** Section A · Shoaib Islam Sir · 🔴 Highest | **Read Time:** ~3 mins
> - **PYQs Covered:** Model Q.1 (2m Def), Model Q.2 (3m Types), Model Q.3 (4m Legal Rules), Model Q.4 (3m Privity & Strangers)
> - **Scoring Rule:** Quote verbatim `Sec 2(d)`, *Currie v. Misa*, cite *Durga Prasad*, *Chinnaya*, and `Sec 25 Expl. 2`.

---

## 📌 1. Verbatim Definitions (For 2-Mark Questions)

> **Consideration [Sec 2(d)]:** "When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise." (Contract Act, 1872)
- *Plain meaning:* The legal price or value exchanged that buys a promise.

> **Consideration (English Common Law):** "A valuable consideration, in the sense of the law, may consist either in some right, interest, profit, or benefit accruing to the one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss, or responsibility, given, suffered, or undertaken by the other." (*Currie v. Misa*, 1875)
- *Plain meaning:* A benefit to one party or a detriment to the other.

> **Quid Pro Quo:** A Latin maxim meaning "something in return" (the mutual exchange of value buying a promise; a bare promise without it is an unenforceable *nudum pactum*).

> **Privity of Contract:** Common law doctrine that only parties to a contract can sue or be sued on it (*Tweddle v. Atkinson*, 1861; *Dunlop v. Selfridge*, 1915). Non-parties cannot enforce agreements.

## ⚡ 2. Core Exam Blocks (Write Directly in Exam)

### Block A: Three Types of Consideration

Consideration is classified into three types based on the timing of performance:

| Type | Timing of Act vs. Promise | Statutory Phrasing [Sec 2(d)] | Bangladesh Status | English Law Status | Practical ECE Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Past** | Act performed before promise is made. | "has done or abstained from doing" | **Valid** under `Sec 2(d)`. | Invalid (*nudum pactum*). | Rewarding an engineer for emergency server recovery last week. |
| **Present (Executed)** | Act performed simultaneously with promise. | "does or abstains from doing" | **Valid**. | **Valid**. | Buying microcontroller chips over the counter with cash. |
| **Future (Executory)** | Act to be performed after promise is made. | "promises to do or to abstain from doing" | **Valid**. | **Valid**. | Agreeing to supply 500 PCBs next month against payment on delivery. |

### Block B: The Seven Legal Rules of Valid Consideration

Valid consideration under the Contract Act 1872 must obey seven fundamental rules:

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 7 Legal Rules of Consideration (D-P-T-R-A-L-D)
> - **D:** Desire of Promisor (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*)
> - **P:** Promisee or Third Person (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*)
> - **T:** Time: Past, Present, Future (`Sec 2(d)`)
> - **R:** Real, not illusory (physically and legally possible)
> - **A:** Adequacy not required (`Sec 25 Expl. 2`)
> - **L:** Lawful object and consideration (`Sec 23`)
> - **D:** Duty: Beyond existing legal or contractual duty

1. **At Promisor's Desire:** Voluntary acts without request create no consideration (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*, 1880).
2. **Move from Promisee or Third Person:** Consideration may move from any third party (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*, 1882).
3. **Past, Present, or Future [Sec 2(d)]:** Valid whether already done, currently moving, or promised for later.
4. **Real and Not Illusory:** Must possess legal value; cannot be physically impossible or legally uncertain.
5. **Need Not Be Adequate [Sec 25 Expl. 2]:** Courts do not judge price fairness. Free consent validates nominal value (selling Tk. 50,000 motor for Tk. 5,000).
6. **Must Be Lawful [Sec 23]:** Cannot be forbidden by law, fraudulent, injurious to person/property, or opposed to public policy.
7. **Beyond Existing Legal Duty:** Fulfilling a pre-existing legal or official duty cannot support a new promise.

### Block C: Stranger to Consideration vs. Stranger to Contract (Privity)

The golden rule of contract enforcement states:
> *"A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue."*

| Parameter | Stranger to Consideration | Stranger to Contract |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | A party to the contract whose consideration was supplied by a third party. | A person who is not a party to the contract. |
| **Contract Party?** | Yes, named as a contracting party. | No, completely external to the agreement. |
| **Right to Sue in BD** | **Can sue** (`Sec 2(d)` allows consideration from "any other person"). | **Cannot sue** (barred by Privity of Contract). |
| **Right to Sue in UK** | Cannot sue (consideration must move from promisee). | Cannot sue. |
| **Leading Case** | *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882) | *Tweddle v. Atkinson* (1861); *Dunlop v. Selfridge* (1915) |
| **Practical Example** | Client A contracts with Firm B; Client's parent pays. B can sue A. | Father contracts with vendor for son's lab. Son cannot sue vendor. |

#### Five Exceptions Where a Stranger to a Contract Can Sue
Equity permits non-parties to enforce contracts in five defined cases:
1. **Trust Beneficiary:** Beneficiary of a trust fund or charge can sue trustees to enforce rights under the trust deed.
2. **Family / Marriage Settlements:** Named relatives can enforce written family partition or marriage maintenance agreements.
3. **Acknowledgment or Estoppel:** A promisor who admits holding funds for a third party becomes directly liable to that party.
4. **Covenants Running with Land:** Land purchasers with notice must obey restrictive covenants attached to the land.
5. **Assignment of Rights:** An assignee of a debt or contractual benefit can sue the debtor in their own name.

## 🎯 3. PYQ & Target Answer Key

### Model Q.1 · Definition of Consideration and Quid Pro Quo (02 Marks)
**Question:** Define Consideration under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872. Explain the concept of *quid pro quo* with an example.

**Answer Blueprint:**
- Quote verbatim `Sec 2(d)` and *Currie v. Misa* from §1.
- Define *quid pro quo* ("something in return") as the price buying a promise. A bare promise (*nudum pactum*) is unenforceable.
- Provide 1-line ECE example: Engineer delivers an oscilloscope; Buyer pays Tk. 60,000. Each performance supports the other.

### Model Q.2 · Three Types of Consideration with Examples (03 Marks)
**Question:** Explain the three types of consideration recognized under the law of contract with suitable examples.

**Answer Blueprint:**
- Draw the comparison table in §2 Block A covering Past, Present, and Future consideration.
- State statutory phrasing from `Sec 2(d)` ("has done", "does", "promises to do").
- Highlight that Bangladesh law enforces past consideration under `Sec 2(d)`, departing from English common law.

### Model Q.3 · Essential Legal Rules Governing Consideration (04 Marks)
**Question:** State and explain the essential legal rules governing valid consideration under the Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer Blueprint:**
- State the 7 rules from §2 Block B using memory hook `D-P-T-R-A-L-D`.
- Cite *Durga Prasad v. Baldeo* (promisor's desire), *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (moves from third party), and `Sec 25 Expl. 2` (adequacy not required).
- Explain that consideration must be real, lawful (`Sec 23`), and beyond existing legal duty.

### Model Q.4 · Privity of Contract and Stranger Rules (03 Marks)
**Question:** Explain the doctrine of Privity of Contract. Discuss: "A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue." State the exceptions.

**Answer Blueprint:**
- State Privity doctrine: only parties can sue (*Tweddle v. Atkinson*, *Dunlop v. Selfridge*).
- Draw the comparison table from §2 Block C contrasting stranger to consideration (*Chinnaya*) vs. stranger to contract.
- List the 5 equity exceptions: Trust beneficiary, Family/marriage settlement, Acknowledgment/estoppel, Covenants running with land, and Assignment of rights.

---

> [!warning] ⚠️ Examiner Traps
> - Past consideration validity: Invalid in English law, but fully valid in Bangladesh under `Sec 2(d)` ("has done").
> - Inadequate consideration: Does not void a contract under `Sec 25 Expl. 2` if consent is free.
> - Confusing strangers: Stranger to consideration CAN sue (*Chinnaya*); stranger to contract CANNOT sue (*Tweddle*; *Dunlop*).
