# L03.1: Consideration Definition, Types, and Rules

> [!info] ⚡ Exam Snapshot
> - **Section & Priority:** Section A · Shoaib Islam Sir · 🔴 Highest Priority
> - **Read Time:** ~3 minutes
> - **Target Questions Covered:** Model Q.1 (2m Definition & Quid Pro Quo), Model Q.2 (3m Types of Consideration), Model Q.3 (4m Legal Rules), Model Q.4 (3m Privity & Stranger Rules)
> - **Scoring Rule:** Quote Section 2(d) verbatim and cite *Currie v. Misa* (1875). State leading case authorities (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*, *Chinnaya v. Ramayya*) and cite Section 25 Explanation 2 for adequacy.

## 📌 1. Verbatim Definitions (For 2-Mark Questions)

> **Consideration [Section 2(d)]:** "When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise." *(The Contract Act, 1872)*

*Plain meaning:* Consideration is the price, benefit, or forbearance exchanged between parties that legally binds a promise.

> **Valuable Consideration (English Common Law):** "A valuable consideration, in the sense of the law, may consist either in some right, interest, profit, or benefit accruing to the one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss, or responsibility, given, suffered, or undertaken by the other." *([Currie v. Misa, 1875](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currie_v_Misa))*

*Plain meaning:* A promise becomes legally enforceable only when one party gains a tangible advantage while the other party incurs a corresponding burden, loss, or legal duty.

> **Quid Pro Quo:** A fundamental Latin legal maxim meaning "something in return." *(The Contract Act, 1872)*

*Plain meaning:* Every valid contract requires a reciprocal exchange of value. A gratuitous, one-sided promise made without any return value is a bare promise (*nudum pactum*) and cannot be enforced in a court of law.

> **Privity of Contract:** The common law doctrine establishing that a contract is a private relationship solely between the parties who formed it. *([Tweddle v. Atkinson, 1861](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweddle_v_Atkinson); [Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co., 1915](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunlop_Pneumatic_Tyre_Co_Ltd_v_Selfridge_%26_Co_Ltd))*

*Plain meaning:* Only an actual party to an agreement can sue or be sued to enforce its terms. A third party who is not a signatory has no legal standing to sue on the agreement.

## ⚡ 2. Core Exam Blocks (Write Directly in Exam)

### Block A: Three Types of Consideration

Under the Contract Act 1872, consideration is classified into three categories based on the temporal relationship between the performance of the act and the making of the promise:

| Consideration Type | Timing of Act vs. Promise | Statutory Phrasing [Sec 2(d)] | Legal Status in Bangladesh | Legal Status in English Law | Practical ECE Industry Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Past** | Act was completed before the promise was made. | "has done or abstained from doing" | **Fully Valid** under Section 2(d). | Generally invalid (*nudum pactum*). | A firm promises a Tk. 10,000 bonus to an engineer for past emergency server recovery. |
| **Present (Executed)** | Act is performed simultaneously with the promise. | "does or abstains from doing" | **Fully Valid**. | **Fully Valid**. | Buying microcontroller units over the counter with immediate cash payment. |
| **Future (Executory)** | Act is to be performed at an agreed future date. | "promises to do or to abstain from doing" | **Fully Valid**. | **Fully Valid**. | A supplier agrees to deliver 500 PCBs next month against payment due within 15 days of delivery. |

---

### Block B: The Seven Legal Rules of Valid Consideration

Valid consideration under the Contract Act 1872 must obey seven fundamental legal rules:

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 7 Legal Rules of Consideration (D-P-T-R-A-L-D)
> - **D:** Desire of Promisor (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*)
> - **P:** Promisee or Third Person (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*)
> - **T:** Time: Past, Present, Future (Section 2(d))
> - **R:** Real, not illusory (physically and legally possible)
> - **A:** Adequacy not required (Section 25, Explanation 2)
> - **L:** Lawful object and consideration (Section 23)
> - **D:** Duty: Beyond existing legal or contractual duty

1. **Must Move at the Desire of the Promisor:**
   - **Legal Rule:** The act or forbearance must be done strictly at the request or desire of the promisor. Voluntary service rendered without an explicit or implied request does not constitute valid consideration.
   - **Leading Case Authority:** *Durga Prasad v. Baldeo* (1880). The plaintiff constructed market stalls at the request of the District Collector. The defendant shopkeepers later promised a commission on goods sold, but failed to pay. The court held the agreement void for lack of consideration, because the stalls were not built at the desire of the shopkeepers.
   - **ECE Application:** If an engineer upgrades a server on personal initiative without prior request, a subsequent promise by the manager to pay a bonus is gratuitous and unenforceable.

2. **May Move from the Promisee or Any Other Person:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Unlike English common law, Bangladesh law does not require consideration to proceed solely from the promisee. A third party may furnish the consideration to validate the contract.
   - **Leading Case Authority:** *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882). A mother gifted landed property to her daughter on the condition that the daughter pay an annual annuity to the maternal aunt. The daughter executed a written agreement promising to pay, but later refused, claiming the aunt gave no consideration. The court rejected the defense, holding that valid consideration moved from the mother.
   - **Practical Rule:** A stranger to consideration has full legal standing to sue and enforce the promise in Bangladesh.

3. **May Be Past, Present, or Future in Time [Section 2(d)]:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Section 2(d) explicitly accommodates past acts ("has done or abstained"), present acts ("does or abstains"), and future promises ("promises to do or to abstain").
   - **Jurisdiction Note:** While English common law treats past consideration as no consideration at all (*nudum pactum*), Bangladesh law recognizes all three temporal categories as equally valid.

4. **Must Be Real, Competent, and Not Illusory:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Consideration must possess real value in the eyes of the law. It cannot be illusory, physically impossible, or legally uncertain.
   - **Disqualifying Examples:** A promise to discover hidden treasure by magic under Section 56 (physically impossible), a promise to avoid criminal prosecution (legally invalid), or a vague promise to pay "a handsome remuneration" (void for uncertainty).

5. **Need Not Be Adequate [Section 25, Explanation 2]:**
   - **Legal Rule:** The law requires the presence of consideration, not its mathematical or market equivalence. Contracting parties are free to determine their own commercial exchange values.
   - **Statutory Authority:** Explanation 2 to Section 25 states that an agreement to which the consent of the promisor is freely given is not void merely because the consideration is inadequate.
   - **Judicial Use:** Inadequacy does not void the contract, but courts may consider severe inadequacy as evidence when assessing whether consent was freely given or obtained through fraud, coercion, or undue influence.

6. **Must Be Lawful [Section 23]:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Under Section 23 of the Contract Act 1872, consideration is void if it is forbidden by law, defeats statutory provisions, is fraudulent, involves injury to person or property, or is regarded by the court as immoral or opposed to public policy.
   - **ECE Application:** An agreement offering financial consideration to leak confidential proprietary telecom firmware is illegal and void *ab initio*.

7. **Must Not Be the Performance of a Pre-Existing Legal Duty:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Doing what a party is already bound to do by public law or by an existing contract cannot support a fresh promise. It confers no new benefit on the promisor and imposes no new detriment on the promisee.
   - **Official Duty Example:** A police officer cannot enforce a private citizen's promise to pay a cash reward for arresting an absconding criminal while on regular duty.
   - **Contractual Exception:** If a contractor undertakes obligations beyond the scope of the original agreement, that additional effort constitutes valid consideration.

### Block C: Stranger to Consideration vs. Stranger to Contract (Privity)

The golden rule of contract enforcement states:

> *"A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue."*

| Parameter | Stranger to Consideration | Stranger to Contract |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | A party to the contract whose consideration was supplied by a third party. | A person who is not a party to the contract. |
| **Party to Agreement?** | Yes, explicitly named as a contracting party. | No, completely external to the agreement. |
| **Right to Sue in Bangladesh** | **Can sue.** Section 2(d) allows consideration to move from "any other person." | **Cannot sue.** Barred by the Doctrine of Privity of Contract. |
| **Right to Sue in English Law** | Cannot sue. English law requires consideration to move from the promisee. | Cannot sue. Privity applies strictly. |
| **Leading Case Authority** | *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882) | *Tweddle v. Atkinson* (1861); *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co.* (1915) |
| **Practical Example** | Client A contracts with Firm B, and Client's parent pays. Firm B can sue Client A. | A father contracts with a builder to wire his son's lab. The son cannot sue the builder directly. |

#### Five Exceptions Where a Stranger to a Contract Can Sue

Equity permits a non-party to enforce a contract in five defined situations:

1. **Beneficiaries under a Trust or Charge:**
   - A third party in whose favor a trust, charge, or specific fund is created by a contract can sue the trustee to enforce those rights.
   - *Example:* A parent transfers company shares to a trustee with instructions to fund an engineering student's tuition. The student can sue the trustee to recover the funds.

2. **Family and Marriage Settlements:**
   - Where a written family arrangement, partition deed, or marriage settlement provides a benefit or maintenance allowance for a female or dependent relative, that person can enforce the agreement despite not being a direct signatory.
   - *Example:* Two brothers executing a partition deed agree to reserve Tk. 300,000 for their sister's wedding expenses. The sister can sue directly to enforce payment.

3. **Acknowledgment or Estoppel:**
   - If a promisor admits, by conduct or written statement, that he holds money or property for the benefit of a third party, he is estopped from denying liability and becomes directly accountable.
   - *Example:* A vendor sends Tk. 50,000 to an intermediary with instructions to pay a hardware subcontractor. The intermediary writes to the subcontractor confirming receipt of the funds. The subcontractor can sue the intermediary directly.

4. **Covenants Running with the Land:**
   - A purchaser who acquires land with notice of restrictive covenants or environmental obligations attached to that land is bound by them, allowing adjoining landowners to sue the new owner directly.
   - *Example:* A title deed restricts property usage to commercial offices. A subsequent purchaser with notice who attempts to construct a noisy fabrication shop can be sued by neighboring property owners.

5. **Assignment of Contractual Rights:**
   - When an assignor legally transfers an actionable claim, debt, or contractual benefit to an assignee, the assignee can sue the debtor directly in their own name.
   - *Example:* A telecommunications equipment supplier assigns outstanding client invoices to a commercial bank. The bank has direct legal standing to sue the client for recovery.

## 🎯 3. PYQ & Target Answer Key

### Model Q.1 · Definition of Consideration and Quid Pro Quo (02 Marks)

**Question:** Define Consideration under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872. Explain the concept of *quid pro quo* with an example.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Statutory Quote:** Quote Section 2(d) of the Contract Act 1872 verbatim: *"When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise."* Support with the English rule from *Currie v. Misa* (1875) defining consideration as a benefit to one party or a detriment to the other.

2. **Concept of Quid Pro Quo:** Explain the Latin maxim *quid pro quo* ("something in return") as the legal price that buys a promise. Emphasize that a one-sided promise without consideration is a bare promise (*nudum pactum*) and cannot be enforced in court.

3. **Applied Example:** Provide a clear engineering transaction: An engineer delivers an oscilloscope worth Tk. 60,000 to a buyer against an agreed payment. The instrument serves as consideration for the payment, while the payment serves as consideration for the instrument.

4. **Concluding Takeaway:** Summarize that reciprocal value is mandatory for legal enforceability under Section 10 and Section 2(d).

### Model Q.2 · Three Types of Consideration with Examples (03 Marks)

**Question:** Explain the three types of consideration recognized under the law of contract with suitable examples.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Classification Basis:** State that consideration is classified into three categories based on the timing of the act relative to the promise under the Contract Act 1872.

2. **Draw Comparison Table:** Reproduce the summary table from §2 Block A covering Past, Present, and Future consideration:
   - **Past Consideration:** Act performed before the promise was made ("has done or abstained"). Valid in Bangladesh under Section 2(d), but invalid in English common law. Example: Rewarding an engineer for emergency server recovery completed last week.
   - **Present (Executed) Consideration:** Act performed simultaneously with the promise ("does or abstains"). Valid in both Bangladesh and English law. Example: Cash purchase of microcontroller chips over the counter.
   - **Future (Executory) Consideration:** Mutual promises to be performed at a future date ("promises to do or to abstain"). Valid in all legal systems. Example: Contract to deliver 500 PCBs next month against payment on delivery.

3. **Statutory Distinction:** Emphasize that the phrasing *"has done or abstained from doing"* in Section 2(d) explicitly validates past consideration in Bangladesh.

4. **Concluding Takeaway:** Conclude that executory consideration constitutes the standard structure for modern commercial and engineering procurement contracts.

### Model Q.3 · Essential Legal Rules Governing Consideration (04 Marks)

**Question:** State and explain the essential legal rules governing valid consideration under the Contract Act, 1872.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Introductory Definition:** Reference Section 2(d) and state that consideration must satisfy seven mandatory statutory rules to validate a contract.

2. **The 7 Rules (Structure via Mnemonic D-P-T-R-A-L-D):**
   - **Rule 1 (Desire of Promisor):** Cite *Durga Prasad v. Baldeo* (1880) to prove voluntary acts without prior request create no consideration.
   - **Rule 2 (Move from Promisee or Third Person):** Cite *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882) confirming that a third party may furnish consideration in Bangladesh.
   - **Rule 3 (Past, Present, or Future):** Cite Section 2(d) statutory phrasing validating all three temporal classes.
   - **Rule 4 (Real and Not Illusory):** Explain that consideration must possess physical and legal possibility, excluding illusory or vague promises.
   - **Rule 5 (Adequacy Not Required):** Cite **Section 25, Explanation 2**, explaining that free consent validates nominal consideration (e.g., selling Tk. 50,000 motor for Tk. 5,000).
   - **Rule 6 (Must Be Lawful):** Cite **Section 23**, excluding illegal, fraudulent, injurious, or immoral objects.
   - **Rule 7 (Beyond Existing Duty):** Clarify that performing an existing legal duty cannot support a fresh promise.

3. **Concluding Takeaway:** Conclude that meeting all seven statutory tests ensures an agreement is an enforceable contract rather than an unenforceable *nudum pactum*.

### Model Q.4 · Privity of Contract and Stranger Rules (03 Marks)

**Question:** Explain the doctrine of Privity of Contract. Discuss: "A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue." State the exceptions.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Define Privity of Contract:** Explain the common law rule that a contract is a private relationship between the parties who made it (*Tweddle v. Atkinson*; *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co.*). A person who is not a party cannot sue or be sued on it.

2. **Differentiate the Two Strangers:** Use the comparison table from §2 Block C to explain the maxim:
   - **Stranger to Consideration:** A party to the contract whose consideration was provided by someone else. Under Section 2(d), this person **can sue** in Bangladesh (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*).
   - **Stranger to Contract:** A person who is not a party to the contract. Barred by privity of contract, this person **cannot sue**, even if the agreement benefits them.

3. **List the Five Equity Exceptions:** Detail the five recognized exceptions where a non-party can sue:
   - Beneficiaries under a trust or charge.
   - Family and marriage settlements.
   - Acknowledgment or estoppel.
   - Covenants running with the land.
   - Assignment of contractual rights.

4. **Concluding Takeaway:** Conclude that while Bangladesh law allows flexibility on who provides consideration, privity of contract remains strictly enforced unless an equitable exception applies.

---

> [!warning] ⚠️ Examiner Traps
> - **Past Consideration Validity:** English common law invalidates past consideration, but Bangladesh law explicitly enforces it under Section 2(d) through the words *"has done or abstained from doing."* Always state this jurisdiction difference clearly.
> - **Inadequacy vs. Invalidity:** Inadequate consideration does not void an agreement if consent was freely given (Section 25, Explanation 2). Never confuse unfair pricing with lack of consideration.
> - **Confusing the Two Strangers:** A stranger to consideration is a contracting party and **can sue** in Bangladesh (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*). A stranger to a contract is an outsider and **cannot sue** (*Dunlop v. Selfridge*), unless one of the five equitable exceptions applies.
