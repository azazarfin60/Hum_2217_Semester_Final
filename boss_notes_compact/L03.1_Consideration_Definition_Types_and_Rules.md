# L03.1: Consideration Definition, Types, and Rules

> [!info] ⚡ Exam Snapshot
> - **Section & Priority:** Section A · Shoaib Islam Sir · 🔴 Highest Priority
> - **Read Time:** ~3 minutes
> - **Target Questions Covered:** Model Q.1 (2m Definition & Quid Pro Quo), Model Q.2 (3m Types of Consideration), Model Q.3 (4m Legal Rules), Model Q.4 (3m Privity & Stranger Rules)
> - **Scoring Rule:** Quote Section 2(d) verbatim and cite *Currie v. Misa* (1875). State leading case authorities (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*, *Chinnaya v. Ramayya*) and cite Section 25 Explanation 2 for adequacy.

---

## 📌 1. Verbatim Definitions (For 2-Mark Questions)

> **Consideration [Section 2(d)]:** "When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise." *(The Contract Act, 1872)*

*Plain meaning:* Consideration is the price you pay for someone else's promise. It can be doing something, giving something, or agreeing not to do something. Without it, a promise is not legally binding.

> **Valuable Consideration (English Common Law):** "A valuable consideration, in the sense of the law, may consist either in some right, interest, profit, or benefit accruing to the one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss, or responsibility, given, suffered, or undertaken by the other." *([Currie v. Misa, 1875](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currie_v_Misa))*

*Plain meaning:* A promise is only enforceable if one person gets a real benefit, while the other takes on a real cost, loss, or duty.

> **Quid Pro Quo:** A fundamental Latin legal maxim meaning "something in return." *(The Contract Act, 1872)*

*Plain meaning:* You give something to get something. A one-sided gift promise has no return value. The law calls it a bare promise (*nudum pactum*) and will not enforce it.

> **Privity of Contract:** The common law doctrine establishing that a contract is a private relationship solely between the parties who formed it. *([Tweddle v. Atkinson, 1861](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweddle_v_Atkinson); [Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co., 1915](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunlop_Pneumatic_Tyre_Co_Ltd_v_Selfridge_%26_Co_Ltd))*

*Plain meaning:* A contract is a private deal only between the people who signed it. Outsiders cannot sue to enforce it, even if the deal was meant to help them.

## ⚡ 2. Core Exam Blocks (Write Directly in Exam)

### Block A: Three Types of Consideration

Under the Contract Act, 1872, consideration is divided into three types based on when the act happens compared to the promise:

| Consideration Type | Timing of Act vs. Promise | Statutory Phrasing [Sec 2(d)] | Legal Status in Bangladesh | Legal Status in English Law | Practical ECE Industry Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Past** | Act finished before the promise was made. | "has done or abstained from doing" | **Fully Valid** under Section 2(d). | Generally invalid (*nudum pactum*). | A firm promises a Tk. 10,000 bonus to an engineer for past emergency server recovery. |
| **Present (Executed)** | Act happens at the same time as the promise. | "does or abstains from doing" | **Fully Valid**. | **Fully Valid**. | Buying microcontroller chips over the counter with immediate cash payment. |
| **Future (Executory)** | Act will happen at an agreed future date. | "promises to do or to abstain from doing" | **Fully Valid**. | **Fully Valid**. | A supplier agrees to deliver 500 PCBs next month against payment due 15 days later. |

---

### Block B: The Seven Legal Rules of Valid Consideration

Valid consideration under the Contract Act, 1872 must follow seven essential legal rules:

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 7 Legal Rules of Consideration (D-P-T-R-A-L-D)
> - **D:** Desire of Promisor (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*)
> - **P:** Promisee or Third Person (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*)
> - **T:** Time: Past, Present, Future (Section 2(d))
> - **R:** Real, not illusory (physically and legally possible)
> - **A:** Adequacy not required (Section 25, Explanation 2)
> - **L:** Lawful object and consideration (Section 23)
> - **D:** Duty: Beyond existing legal or contractual duty

1. **Must Move at the Desire of the Promisor:**
   - **Legal Rule:** The act must be done strictly because the promisor asked for it. Voluntary help done without an explicit or implied request does not count as valid consideration.
   - **Leading Case Authority:** *Durga Prasad v. Baldeo* (1880). A builder constructed market stalls because the District Collector requested it. Later, local merchants promised to pay the builder a sales commission. They never paid. The court ruled the promise was void. The builder did the work for the Collector, not at the merchants' desire.
   - **ECE Application:** An engineer tunes up a company server over the weekend without being asked. If the manager later promises a Tk. 5,000 bonus, that promise is purely voluntary and cannot be enforced in court.

2. **May Move from the Promisee or Any Other Person:**
   - **Legal Rule:** In Bangladesh, the payment or benefit does not have to come from the person signing the contract. A third party can provide the consideration.
   - **Leading Case Authority:** *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882). A mother gifted land to her daughter. In return, the daughter signed an agreement promising to pay an annual allowance to her maternal aunt. Later, the daughter stopped paying. She claimed the aunt never gave her any consideration. The court rejected her excuse. The contract was valid because the mother provided the consideration.
   - **Practical Rule:** In Bangladesh, a stranger to consideration has full legal standing to sue and enforce the promise.

3. **May Be Past, Present, or Future in Time [Section 2(d)]:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Section 2(d) covers all three timeframes. It explicitly validates past acts ("has done"), current acts ("does"), and future promises ("promises to do").
   - **Key Law Difference:** English common law treats past consideration as no consideration at all (*nudum pactum*). Bangladesh law accepts past, present, and future consideration as fully valid.

4. **Must Be Real, Competent, and Not Illusory:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Consideration must have genuine value in the eyes of the law. It cannot be imaginary, physically impossible, or legally vague.
   - **Examples of Void Deals:** Promising to find hidden treasure by magic under Section 56 is physically impossible. Promising not to file a criminal complaint is legally void. Promising to pay "a handsome reward" is too vague to enforce.

5. **Need Not Be Adequate [Section 25, Explanation 2]:**
   - **Legal Rule:** The law requires some consideration, but it does not care if the deal is economically fair. The parties are free to agree on their own prices.
   - **Statutory Authority:** Explanation 2 to Section 25 states that an agreement with freely given consent is not void merely because the consideration is inadequate.
   - **Judicial Use:** A very low price does not void a contract on its own. However, courts may review low prices as evidence to see if consent was forced, tricked, or coerced.

6. **Must Be Lawful [Section 23]:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Consideration must be lawful. Under Section 23, consideration is void if it is forbidden by law, defeats statutory rules, or involves fraud. It is also void if it injures others or violates public policy.
   - **ECE Application:** Paying an engineer Tk. 50,000 to steal trade secret telecom firmware is illegal. The agreement is void from day one (*void ab initio*).

7. **Must Not Be the Performance of a Pre-Existing Legal Duty:**
   - **Legal Rule:** Doing what you are already legally required to do cannot support a fresh promise. It gives no new benefit to the promisor and causes no new sacrifice for the promisee.
   - **Official Duty Example:** A police officer cannot enforce a citizen's cash reward for arresting a criminal during regular duty hours.
   - **Contractual Exception:** If a contractor does extra work beyond the original contract specs, that extra work counts as valid consideration.

### Block C: Stranger to Consideration vs. Stranger to Contract (Privity)

The golden rule of contract enforcement states:

> *"A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue."*

*Plain meaning:* The payment can come from a third person like an aunt, and the contract is still valid in Bangladesh. But if you were not a party who signed the deal, you usually cannot sue on it.

| Parameter | Stranger to Consideration | Stranger to Contract |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | A signed party to the deal whose payment was provided by someone else. | An outsider who never signed or joined the contract. |
| **Party to Agreement?** | Yes. Named as a direct party in the agreement. | No. Completely outside the agreement. |
| **Right to Sue in Bangladesh** | **Can sue.** Section 2(d) says consideration can move from "any other person." | **Cannot sue.** Blocked by the Doctrine of Privity of Contract. |
| **Right to Sue in English Law** | Cannot sue. English law requires payment to come directly from the promisee. | Cannot sue. Privity applies strictly. |
| **Leading Case Authority** | *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882) | *Tweddle v. Atkinson* (1861); *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co.* (1915) |
| **Simple Example** | Client A hires Firm B, and Client A's parent pays the bill. Firm B can sue Client A. | A father hires an electrician to wire his son's lab. The son cannot sue the electrician if the wiring fails. |

#### Five Exceptions Where a Stranger to a Contract Can Sue

Equity courts permit a non-party to enforce a contract in five specific situations:

1. **Beneficiaries under a Trust or Charge:**
   - A trust sets aside money or property for someone's benefit. That beneficiary can sue the trustee to get the funds, even without signing the trust deed.
   - *Example:* A parent transfers company shares to a trustee to pay an engineering student's tuition. The student can sue the trustee to recover the money.

2. **Family and Marriage Settlements:**
   - Family members may divide property in writing and reserve money for a female or dependent relative. That relative can enforce the agreement directly.
   - *Example:* Two brothers split family land and sign a deed reserving Tk. 300,000 for their sister's wedding. The sister can sue them directly to get the money.

3. **Acknowledgment or Estoppel:**
   - A person may admit in writing or by conduct that they hold money for an outsider. Once they admit it, they cannot deny liability later.
   - *Example:* A client pays an agent Tk. 50,000 to deliver to an ECE lab subcontractor. The agent writes to the subcontractor confirming receipt of the funds. The subcontractor can sue the agent directly.

4. **Covenants Running with the Land:**
   - A person who buys land knowing it has building restrictions must obey those rules. Neighboring landowners can sue the new owner directly to enforce them.
   - *Example:* A deed restricts land use to commercial offices. If a buyer tries to build a noisy factory, neighboring owners can sue directly to stop construction.

5. **Assignment of Contractual Rights:**
   - A company can legally transfer its unpaid bills or debt claims to someone else. The new owner can sue the debtor directly in their own name.
   - *Example:* A telecom equipment supplier assigns outstanding client invoices to a bank. The bank has direct legal standing to sue the client for payment.

## 🎯 3. PYQ & Target Answer Key

### Model Q.1 · Definition of Consideration and Quid Pro Quo (02 Marks)

**Question:** Define Consideration under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872. Explain the concept of *quid pro quo* with an example.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Statutory Quote:**
   - Quote Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872 verbatim.
   - Cite *Currie v. Misa* (1875). Consideration is a benefit to one party or a detriment to the other.

2. **Concept of Quid Pro Quo:**
   - Explain the Latin phrase *quid pro quo* ("something in return").
   - It is the legal price that buys a promise.
   - A one-sided gift promise without return value is a bare promise (*nudum pactum*). Courts will not enforce it.

3. **Applied Example:**
   - Use an engineering trade. An engineer delivers an oscilloscope worth Tk. 60,000 to a buyer.
   - The device is consideration for the cash. The cash is consideration for the device.

4. **Concluding Takeaway:**
   - Reciprocal exchange of value is mandatory for legal enforceability under Section 10 and Section 2(d).

### Model Q.2 · Three Types of Consideration with Examples (03 Marks)

**Question:** Explain the three types of consideration recognized under the law of contract with suitable examples.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Classification Basis:**
   - Consideration is split into three classes based on timing under the Contract Act, 1872.
   - It depends on when the act happens relative to the promise.

2. **Draw Summary Table:**
   - Reproduce the summary table from §2 Block A covering Past, Present, and Future consideration.
   - **Past Consideration:** The act finished before the promise was made ("has done or abstained"). Valid in Bangladesh under Section 2(d), but void in English law. Example: Promising a Tk. 10,000 bonus for emergency server work done last week.
   - **Present (Executed) Consideration:** The act and promise happen at the same time ("does or abstains"). Valid in both legal systems. Example: Paying cash for microcontrollers at a retail counter.
   - **Future (Executory) Consideration:** Both sides exchange promises to be performed later ("promises to do or abstain"). Valid in all legal systems. Example: Agreeing to deliver 500 PCBs next month against payment upon delivery.

3. **Statutory Distinction:**
   - Highlight the phrase *"has done or abstained from doing"* in Section 2(d). This phrasing explicitly validates past consideration in Bangladesh.

4. **Concluding Takeaway:**
   - Executory consideration is the standard model for modern engineering and supply contracts.

### Model Q.3 · Essential Legal Rules Governing Consideration (04 Marks)

**Question:** State and explain the essential legal rules governing valid consideration under the Contract Act, 1872.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Introductory Definition:**
   - Reference Section 2(d).
   - Consideration must satisfy seven strict statutory rules to validate an agreement.

2. **The 7 Rules (Structure via Mnemonic D-P-T-R-A-L-D):**
   - **Rule 1 (Desire of Promisor):** Cite *Durga Prasad v. Baldeo* (1880). Doing voluntary work without a request creates no legal consideration.
   - **Rule 2 (Move from Promisee or Third Person):** Cite *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882). In Bangladesh, a third party can provide consideration.
   - **Rule 3 (Past, Present, or Future):** Cite Section 2(d). All three timeframes are fully valid in Bangladesh.
   - **Rule 4 (Real and Not Illusory):** Consideration must be physically and legally possible. Magic or vague promises do not count.
   - **Rule 5 (Adequacy Not Required):** Cite Section 25, Explanation 2. Free consent makes nominal pricing valid, like selling a Tk. 50,000 motor for Tk. 5,000.
   - **Rule 6 (Must Be Lawful):** Cite Section 23. Consideration cannot be illegal, fraudulent, harmful, or immoral.
   - **Rule 7 (Beyond Existing Duty):** Doing what you are already legally bound to do creates no fresh consideration.

3. **Concluding Takeaway:**
   - Meeting all seven statutory tests ensures an agreement is an enforceable contract rather than a void *nudum pactum*.

### Model Q.4 · Privity of Contract and Stranger Rules (03 Marks)

**Question:** Explain the doctrine of Privity of Contract. Discuss: "A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue." State the exceptions.

**Exam Writing Plan:**

1. **Define Privity of Contract:**
   - A contract is a private deal between the parties who made it (*Tweddle v. Atkinson*; *Dunlop v. Selfridge*).
   - An outsider cannot sue to enforce it, even if the agreement benefits them.

2. **Differentiate the Two Strangers:**
   - Use the comparison table from §2 Block C to explain the maxim:
   - **Stranger to Consideration:** They signed the deal, but someone else paid for them. Under Section 2(d), they **can sue** in Bangladesh (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*).
   - **Stranger to Contract:** They are an outsider who never signed the deal. Under privity of contract, they **cannot sue**.

3. **List the Five Equity Exceptions:**
   - Beneficiaries under a trust or charge.
   - Family and marriage settlements.
   - Acknowledgment or estoppel.
   - Covenants running with the land.
   - Assignment of contractual rights.

4. **Concluding Takeaway:**
   - Bangladesh allows third parties to pay, but keeps the door closed to non-signatories unless an equity exception applies.

---

> [!warning] ⚠️ Examiner Traps
> - **Past Consideration Validity:** English law rejects past consideration. In contrast, Bangladesh law explicitly enforces it under Section 2(d) through the words *"has done or abstained from doing."* Always highlight this jurisdiction difference.
> - **Inadequacy vs. Invalidity:** Inadequate consideration does not void an agreement if consent was freely given (Section 25, Explanation 2). Never confuse unfair pricing with lack of consideration.
> - **Confusing the Two Strangers:** A stranger to consideration is a contracting party and **can sue** in Bangladesh (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*). A stranger to a contract is an outsider and **cannot sue** (*Dunlop v. Selfridge*), unless one of the five equitable exceptions applies.
