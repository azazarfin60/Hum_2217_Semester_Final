## L03.1 · Definition of Consideration and Quid Pro Quo

*No past final or class test set this topic directly. Shoaib Sir marked all of Chapter 2 as high-yield for 2026.*

### L03.1 · Model Exam Question 1 · 02 Marks

*Suggested Topic (Chapter 2) · Target: Part (a)*

**Question.** Define Consideration under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872. Explain the concept of *quid pro quo* with an example.

**Answer.**

**1. Statutory Definition of Consideration:**
Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872 defines consideration as follows:
"When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise."

In *Currie v. Misa* (1875), the court gave a classic definition. Consideration is a benefit to one party or a detriment to the other.

**2. Concept of Quid Pro Quo:**
*Quid pro quo* is a Latin phrase meaning "something in return". Consideration represents the price paid for a promise. In every valid contract, each party must give something and receive something. A promise to do or give something for nothing is a bare promise. The law does not enforce bare promises.

**3. Example:**
P agrees to sell an oscilloscope to Q for Tk. 50,000.
- For P's promise to deliver the oscilloscope, the consideration is Tk. 50,000.
- For Q's promise to pay Tk. 50,000, the consideration is the oscilloscope.
Both parties gain a benefit and undertake an obligation.

## L03.2 · Types of Consideration

*No past final or class test set this topic directly. Shoaib Sir marked all of Chapter 2 as high-yield for 2026.*

### L03.2 · Model Exam Question 1 · 03 Marks

*Suggested Topic (Chapter 2) · Target: Part (b)*

**Question.** Explain the three types of consideration recognized under the law of contract with suitable examples.

**Answer.**

Consideration is classified into three types based on the time of performance:

**1. Past Consideration:**
- **Definition:** The act or forbearance was performed before the promise was made. The promisor later promises compensation for that prior act.
- **Legal Status:** Under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872, past consideration is valid in Bangladesh. The definition covers an act where the promisee "has done or abstained from doing" something.
- **Example:** X repairs Y's laptop in January without requesting payment. In February, Y promises to pay X Tk. 2,000 for that service. X's service is past consideration. Y's promise is legally binding.

**2. Present (Executed) Consideration:**
- **Definition:** Consideration moves simultaneously with the promise. One party performs their obligation at the exact moment the contract is made.
- **Example:** A buys microcontrollers at a retail store and pays cash immediately at the counter. A's cash payment is present or executed consideration.

**3. Future (Executory) Consideration:**
- **Definition:** Consideration moves at a future date. Both parties exchange mutual promises to perform acts in the future. A promise on one side supports a promise on the other side.
- **Example:** Walton agrees to deliver 100 LCD panels to an assembly plant next month. The plant agrees to pay Tk. 300,000 upon delivery. Both obligations remain executory until performed.

## L03.3 · Legal Rules Regarding Valid Consideration

*No past paper had this standalone question. Shoaib Sir taught these rules in Class 3 and marked them high-yield.*

### L03.3 · Model Exam Question 1 · 04 Marks

*Suggested Topic (Chapter 2) · Target: Part (b) or (c)*

**Question.** State and explain the essential legal rules governing valid consideration under the Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer.**

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 7 Legal Rules of Consideration
> Remember the sentence: **"Do Promise To Receive All Lawful Deals"**
> - **D**esire: Must move at the desire of the promisor
> - **P**romisee or stranger: May move from any third person
> - **T**ime: May be past, present, or future (Sec 2(d))
> - **R**eal: Must have legal value and not be illusory
> - **A**dequacy: Consideration need not be adequate (Sec 25 Expl. 2)
> - **L**awful: Must not be illegal, immoral, or against public policy (Sec 23)
> - **D**uty: Performance of a pre-existing legal duty is invalid

A valid consideration must satisfy the following seven legal rules:

**1. Must move at the desire of the promisor:**
The act or forbearance must happen at the request of the promisor. A voluntary act done without request does not count as consideration.
- *Example:* P saves Q's goods from a factory fire without Q asking for help. P cannot demand payment because Q never requested the service (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*).

**2. May move from the promisee or any other person:**
Under Section 2(d), consideration need not move from the promisee alone. It may move from a third person. A stranger to consideration can enforce the contract.
- *Example:* In *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882), a mother gifted land to her daughter. She directed the daughter to pay an annual allowance to her maternal uncle. The daughter agreed in writing with the uncle. The court held that the uncle could enforce payment. Consideration moved from the mother, but the agreement remained enforceable.

**3. May be past, present, or future (Section 2(d)):**
Section 2(d) covers acts already done, acts currently performed, and mutual promises for the future. Past, present, and future consideration are all recognized under Bangladesh law.
- *Example:* A merchant may accept immediate cash, a promise of payment next week, or prior repair services as consideration.

**4. Must be real and not illusory:**
Consideration must have value in the eyes of the law. It cannot be physically impossible, legally impossible, or uncertain.
- *Example:* A promise to discover treasure by magic is void. It lacks real consideration.

**5. Need not be adequate (Section 25, Explanation 2):**
The law does not require consideration to match the full market value of the promise. The contracting parties decide the value themselves. An agreement with free consent is valid even if the consideration is small.
- *Example:* Selling an electric motor worth Tk. 100,000 for Tk. 5,000 with free consent creates a valid contract. But the court may examine severe inadequacy to check if consent was truly free.

**6. Must be lawful (Section 23):**
The consideration must not be illegal, immoral, or opposed to public policy. An agreement with an unlawful consideration is void.
- *Example:* An agreement to pay money to forge an academic certificate is void. The consideration is illegal.

**7. Must not be a pre-existing legal duty:**
Doing what a person is already bound to do by law or official duty is not valid consideration. It creates no new benefit or legal detriment.
- *Example:* A police officer cannot claim a cash reward for arresting an offender within routine official duty.

### L03.3 · Model Exam Question 2 · 03 Marks

*Suggested Topic (Chapter 2) · Target: Part (b)*

**Question.** Explain the doctrine of Privity of Contract. Discuss the statement: "A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue." State the exceptions to this doctrine.

**Answer.**

**1. Doctrine of Privity of Contract:**
Privity of contract means that only parties to a contract can enforce it. A person who is not a party cannot sue on the contract. This rule holds even if the contract was made for their benefit (*Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co.*).

**2. Stranger to Consideration vs. Stranger to Contract:**
- **Stranger to consideration:** Under Section 2(d), consideration may move from a third person. A stranger to consideration can sue as long as they are a party to the contract (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*).
- **Stranger to contract:** A stranger to the contract has no contractual relationship with the promisor. Such a person cannot sue.
So a stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue.

**3. Exceptions (Where a Stranger to a Contract Can Sue):**
A person who is not a party to a contract can sue in the following exceptional cases:
1. **Trust or Beneficiary:** The beneficiary of a trust can sue the trustee. They can enforce their rights without being a party to the trust deed.
2. **Family and Marriage Settlements:** Agreements for marriage expenses or family maintenance protect specific relatives. The intended beneficiary can enforce these terms directly.
3. **Acknowledgment or Estoppel:** The promisor may admit holding money for a third party. That third party can then sue to recover the money.
4. **Covenants Running with Land:** A buyer may acquire land with notice of attached obligations. The affected party can enforce those obligations against the buyer.
5. **Assignment of Contract:** An assignee of contractual rights can sue the debtor directly. Transferred insurance policies are a common example.

## L03.4 · Exceptions to No Consideration No Contract

*No past paper set this standalone question. Shoaib Sir marked all of Chapter 2 as high-yield for 2026.*

### L03.4 · Model Exam Question 1 · 04 Marks

*Suggested Topic (Chapter 2) · Target: Part (b) or (c)*

**Question.** "An agreement made without consideration is void." Discuss this rule (*Ex nudo pacto non oritur actio*) under Section 25 of the Contract Act, 1872. Explain the statutory exceptions to this rule.

**Answer.**

**1. General Rule and Legal Maxim:**
Section 25 of the Contract Act, 1872 states that an agreement made without consideration is void. This rule comes from the Latin maxim *Ex nudo pacto non oritur actio*. The phrase means that no right of action arises from a bare agreement. Consideration is essential to create a binding contract. Without consideration, a promise is purely gratuitous and creates no legal obligation.

**2. Statutory Exceptions to the Rule:**
The Contract Act, 1872 provides seven exceptions where an agreement without consideration is valid and enforceable:

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 7 Exceptions to "No Consideration, No Contract"
> Remember the sentence: **"Loving Volunteers Take All Generous Gifts & Charity"**
> - **L**oving: Natural **L**ove and affection [Sec 25(1)]
> - **V**olunteers: Past **V**oluntary service compensation [Sec 25(2)]
> - **T**ake: **T**ime-barred debt in writing [Sec 25(3)]
> - **A**ll: **A**gency creation without consideration [Sec 185]
> - **G**enerous: **G**ifts actually completed [Sec 25 Expl. 1]
> - **G**ifts: Contract of **G**uarantee [Sec 127]
> - **C**harity: **C**haritable subscription with detriment

**1. Natural Love and Affection [Section 25(1)]:**
An agreement made without consideration is valid if it meets four statutory conditions:
- It is expressed in writing.
- It is registered under the law for document registration.
- It is made on account of natural love and affection.
- It is between parties standing in a near relation to each other.
- *Example:* A father promises in a written and registered deed to give his son Tk. 100,000 out of love. The agreement is valid.

**2. Compensation for Past Voluntary Service [Section 25(2)]:**
A person may voluntarily do something for the promisor without any prior request. A later promise to compensate that person is legally binding under Section 25(2). This rule also covers acts the promisor was legally bound to do.
- *Example:* X finds Y's lost wallet and returns it. Y then promises to give X Tk. 1,000. This promise is legally binding.

**3. Promise to Pay a Time-Barred Debt [Section 25(3)]:**
A debt barred by the law of limitation cannot be recovered in court. But a promise to pay such a debt is enforceable if:
- It is made in writing.
- It is signed by the debtor or their authorized agent.
- *Example:* A owes B Tk. 50,000, but the debt is barred by limitation. A signs a written promise to pay Tk. 30,000. B can enforce this promise.

**4. Agency [Section 185]:**
Section 185 states that no consideration is required to create an agency. An agent is legally appointed without consideration.

**5. Completed Gifts [Section 25, Explanation 1]:**
The rule does not apply to completed gifts. Explanation 1 to Section 25 protects gifts actually made between donor and donee. Once a gift is accepted and delivered, the donor cannot demand it back for lack of consideration.

**6. Contract of Guarantee [Section 127]:**
A contract of guarantee needs no direct consideration between the creditor and the surety. Under Section 127, any benefit given to the principal debtor serves as sufficient consideration for the surety.

**7. Charitable Subscription with Detriment:**
A bare promise to donate money to charity is void. But the promise becomes enforceable if the charity incurs debt relying on that pledge (*Kedar Nath v. Gorie Mohammad*).
