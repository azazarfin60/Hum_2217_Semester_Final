## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Consideration (Statutory Definition):** "When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise." (Contract Act, 1872, Section 2(d))

Consideration is what each party gives or gives up. It is the legal price that buys a promise.

> **Consideration (English Common Law):** "A valuable consideration, in the sense of the law, may consist either in some right, interest, profit, or benefit accruing to the one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss, or responsibility, given, suffered, or undertaken by the other." (*Currie v. Misa*, 1875)

One party gains an advantage, while the other party undertakes a burden or duty.

> **Quid Pro Quo:** A Latin phrase meaning "something in return".

In every valid contract, both sides must give something and receive something. A one-sided gift is not a contract.

> **Privity of Contract:** The legal doctrine that only parties to a contract can sue or be sued on it.

A stranger to a contract cannot sue. But under Bangladesh law, a stranger to consideration can sue.

## The Core Concept of Consideration

### The Principle of Quid Pro Quo

Contracts are commercial bargains, not gifts. The law does not enforce promises made for nothing. A bare promise is called a *nudum pactum*. 

To make a promise binding, the promisor must receive something in return. That return is consideration (*quid pro quo*).

Consideration can take two forms:
1. **A positive act:** Doing something that the party is not already bound to do.
2. **A negative abstinence (forbearance):** Refraining from doing something that the party has a legal right to do.

- *ECE Example:* Firm A promises to pay Tk. 50,000 to Engineer B. In return, Engineer B designs a printed circuit board.
  - For Firm A's promise, the consideration is B's design work.
  - For Engineer B's work, the consideration is Firm A's payment of Tk. 50,000.
  - Both parties gain a benefit and take on a duty.

### Statutory Breakdown of Section 2(d)

Section 2(d) contains four key elements:
1. **At the desire of the promisor:** The act must occur because the promisor asked for it. Voluntary help without a request is not consideration.
2. **By the promisee or any other person:** Consideration does not have to come from the promisee alone. A third party may provide it.
3. **Time of the act or abstinence:**
   - Has done or abstained from doing (Past consideration).
   - Does or abstains from doing (Present consideration).
   - Promises to do or abstain from doing (Future consideration).
4. **An act, abstinence, or promise:** The act or forbearance is the consideration itself.

## Three Types of Consideration

Consideration is classified into three types based on the time when the act is performed.

### 1. Past Consideration

The act or service was performed before the promise was made. The promisor later promises to pay or reward that earlier service.

- **Status in Bangladesh:** Fully valid under Section 2(d). The words "has done or abstained from doing" explicitly cover past acts.
- **Status in English Law:** Past consideration is generally not valid in English common law. Bangladesh law departs from English law here.
- *Example:* X fixes Y's broken laboratory power supply in January without asking for payment. In February, Y promises to pay X Tk. 3,000 for that repair. X's past service supports Y's promise. Y is legally bound to pay.

### 2. Present or Executed Consideration

Consideration moves at the exact same time the promise is made. One party completes performance immediately upon agreement.

- **Status:** Fully valid in both Bangladesh and English law.
- *Example:* A student buys an STM32 development board at an electronics store. The student hands cash over the counter, and the shopkeeper hands over the board. The payment is present consideration.

### 3. Future or Executory Consideration

Both parties exchange mutual promises to perform acts at a later date. Neither party performs immediately. A promise on one side supports a promise on the other side.

- **Status:** Fully valid and very common in commercial transactions.
- *Example:* Walton agrees to supply 500 display panels to an assembly plant next month. The plant promises to pay Tk. 1,500,000 within 15 days of delivery. Both obligations are executory until performed.

### Comparison of the Three Types of Consideration

| Comparison Point | Past Consideration | Present (Executed) Consideration | Future (Executory) Consideration |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Timing of Act** | Performed before the promise is made. | Performed at the exact time of the contract. | To be performed at a future date. |
| **Timing of Promise** | Made after the act has already occurred. | Made simultaneously with the act. | Made before either party performs. |
| **Statutory Words in Sec 2(d)** | "has done or abstained from doing" | "does or abstains from doing" | "promises to do or to abstain from doing" |
| **Validity in Bangladesh** | Valid under Section 2(d). | Valid under Section 2(d). | Valid under Section 2(d). |
| **Validity in English Law** | Generally invalid (past consideration is no consideration). | Valid. | Valid. |
| **ECE Industry Example** | Rewarding an engineer for past emergency server recovery. | Buying electronic chips with instant cash payment. | Signing a supply contract for microcontrollers due next quarter. |

## The Seven Legal Rules of Valid Consideration

To support a valid contract, consideration must follow seven strict statutory rules.

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 7 Legal Rules of Consideration
> Remember this simple phrase: **"Do Promise To Receive All Lawful Deals"**
> - **D**esire: Must move at the desire of the promisor
> - **P**romisee or stranger: May move from any third person
> - **T**ime: May be past, present, or future (`Sec 2(d)`)
> - **R**eal: Must have legal value and not be illusory
> - **A**dequacy: Consideration need not be adequate (`Sec 25 Expl. 2`)
> - **L**awful: Must not be illegal, immoral, or against public policy (`Sec 23`)
> - **D**uty: Performance of a pre-existing legal duty is invalid

### Rule 1: Move at the Desire of the Promisor

The act or forbearance must be done at the request of the promisor. Voluntary acts done without any request do not count as consideration.

- *Leading Case:* *Durga Prasad v. Baldeo* (1880). The plaintiff built shops in a market at the request of the District Collector. Later, shopkeepers promised to pay the plaintiff a commission on sales. The shopkeepers failed to pay, and the plaintiff sued. The court held the promise void. The shops were built for the Collector, not at the desire of the shopkeepers.
- *ECE Example:* An engineer notices a faulty network router at a friend's office and replaces its capacitors voluntarily. The friend later promises to pay Tk. 2,000. If the engineer acted without any prior request, he cannot sue for payment.

### Rule 2: Move from Promisee or Any Other Person

In Bangladesh law, consideration does not have to come from the promisee. It can come from a third person. As long as consideration exists, the promise is binding.

- *Leading Case:* *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882). An elderly lady gifted landed property to her daughter. The gift deed directed the daughter to pay an annual allowance to the lady's sister (the maternal aunt). On the same day, the daughter signed a written agreement with the aunt promising to pay the allowance. Later, the daughter refused to pay. She argued that the aunt gave no consideration. The court rejected her argument. Consideration moved from the mother. That was enough to bind the daughter.
- *Rule Summary:* A stranger to consideration can sue in Bangladesh.

### Rule 3: Past, Present, or Future in Time

Under Section 2(d), consideration may be past, present, or future.
- It covers acts already done before the promise.
- It covers acts done right at the time of the promise.
- It covers promises to do acts in the future.

### Rule 4: Real and Not Illusory

Consideration must have real value in the eyes of the law. It cannot be physically impossible, legally impossible, or uncertain.

- *Physical Impossibility:* A promises to walk on water for Tk. 10,000. Void.
- *Legal Impossibility:* A promises to pay B Tk. 5,000 if B commits a crime. Void.
- *Uncertain Consideration:* A promises to pay B "a reasonable sum" for unspecified advice. Void for vagueness.
- *Illusory Acts:* A promise to discover hidden treasure by magic under Section 56 is void.

### Rule 5: Consideration Need Not Be Adequate

The law does not require consideration to match the full market value of the good or service. The contracting parties decide the price for themselves.

Explanation 2 to Section 25 explicitly states:
> *"An agreement to which the consent of the promisor is freely given is not void merely because the consideration is inadequate."*

- *Example:* A agrees to sell an oscilloscope worth Tk. 100,000 for only Tk. 5,000. If A gives free consent, the contract is fully valid.
- *Court's View:* The court does not make bargains for parties. If a party claims fraud or undue influence, the court checks severe inadequacy. This helps verify if consent was truly free.

### Rule 6: Must Be Lawful

Under Section 23 of the Contract Act, 1872, consideration must not be:
1. Forbidden by law.
2. Defeating the provisions of any law.
3. Fraudulent.
4. Involving injury to person or property.
5. Immoral or opposed to public policy.

An agreement with unlawful consideration is void.

- *Example:* A promises B Tk. 20,000 to leak proprietary telecom source code. The consideration involves an illegal breach of trade secrets. The agreement is void.

### Rule 7: Must Not Be a Pre-Existing Legal Duty

Doing what a person is already legally required to do is not valid consideration. Doing an existing duty gives no new benefit to the promisor and causes no new detriment to the promisee.

- *Official Public Duty:* A police officer cannot claim a private reward for arresting an offender on regular duty.
- *Pre-Existing Contractual Duty:* A contractor agreed to deliver a server rack by Friday. He cannot demand extra pay just to finish on time.
- *Exception:* If the party does something extra beyond the call of duty, that extra effort is valid consideration.

## Privity of Contract and Stranger Rules

### Doctrine of Privity of Contract

The Doctrine of Privity of Contract means that a contract is a private relationship between the parties who made it.

The core rules of Privity are:
1. Only a person who is a party to a contract can sue on it.
2. A contract cannot impose burdens or liabilities on a person who is not a party.
3. Even if a contract was made for the benefit of a third person, that third person cannot sue.

- *Leading English Case:* *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co.* (1915). Dunlop sold tires to a distributor, Dew & Co., on terms that Dew would not sell below list prices. Dew sold tires to Selfridge, who agreed not to sell below list prices. Selfridge sold below list price. Dunlop sued Selfridge. The court held that Dunlop could not sue Selfridge. Dunlop was not a party to the contract between Dew and Selfridge.

### Stranger to Consideration vs Stranger to Contract

Students often confuse these two concepts. You must distinguish them clearly in the exam.

- **Stranger to Consideration:** A party to the agreement whose consideration was provided by someone else. In Bangladesh, a stranger to consideration **can sue** (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*).
- **Stranger to Contract:** A person who is not a party to the agreement at all. A stranger to a contract **cannot sue**, even if the contract was made for his benefit.

> [!tip] Memory Hook: The Golden Maxim
> *"A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue."*

### Comparison: Stranger to Consideration vs Stranger to Contract

| Comparison Parameter | Stranger to Consideration | Stranger to Contract |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Meaning** | A party to the contract who did not personally provide the consideration. | A person who has no contract with the promisor. |
| **Party to Agreement?** | Yes. His name appears as a contracting party. | No. He is completely outside the contract. |
| **Right to Sue in Bangladesh** | **Can sue.** Section 2(d) allows consideration from "any other person". | **Cannot sue.** Privity of contract bars external lawsuits. |
| **Leading Case** | *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* (1882) | *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co.* (1915) |
| **Right to Sue in English Law** | Cannot sue. English law requires consideration to move from the promisee. | Cannot sue. Privity applies strictly. |
| **Practical Example** | A daughter agrees with her aunt to pay an allowance using money given by the mother. The aunt can sue. | A father contracts with a builder to build a lab for his son. The son cannot sue the builder directly. |

### Five Exceptions Where a Stranger to a Contract Can Sue

In five special situations, equity allows a stranger to a contract to file a lawsuit:

1. **Beneficiaries under a Trust or Charge:**
   A contract may create a trust or fund for a third person. That beneficiary can sue the trustee to enforce the trust.
   - *Example:* A father transfers property to a trustee to pay for his daughter's education. The daughter can sue the trustee to receive the funds.

2. **Family and Marriage Settlements:**
   When family members execute a written agreement for marriage expenses or partition maintenance, the named family member can enforce it.
   - *Example:* Two brothers agree on family property partition to invest Tk. 200,000 for their sister's wedding. The sister can sue them to enforce the term.

3. **Acknowledgment or Estoppel:**
   A promisor may admit that he holds money for a third party. He is then estopped from denying liability.
   - *Example:* A sends Tk. 10,000 to B to pay C. B writes to C saying he holds Tk. 10,000 for C. C can sue B to recover the money.

4. **Covenants Running with Land:**
   A person who buys land with notice of duties or restrictions attached to that land is bound by those terms. The affected party can sue the new owner.
   - *Example:* An owner agrees not to build a factory on a plot to protect adjoining residential land. A buyer with notice must obey the rule.

5. **Assignment of Contractual Rights:**
   When a creditor assigns his right to collect a debt to an assignee, the assignee can sue the debtor directly.
   - *Example:* An electronics supplier assigns unpaid client invoices to a bank. The bank can sue the client to recover the payment.

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive & Model Answers)

### 🎯 GQ1 · Model Exam Question 1 · Definition of Consideration and Quid Pro Quo (02 Marks)

> **Appeared:** Prescribed Model Question · Chapter 2 Target Part (a) · (02 Marks)

**Question:**
Define Consideration under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872. Explain the concept of *quid pro quo* with an example.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Statutory Definition of Consideration

Under **Section 2(d)** of the Contract Act, 1872:
> *"When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise."*

In *Currie v. Misa* (1875), the court defined consideration as a benefit to one party or a detriment to the other party.

#### 2. Concept of Quid Pro Quo

*Quid pro quo* is a Latin maxim meaning "something in return". It represents the price demanded by the promisor for his promise. Every valid contract requires an exchange of value. A promise to do or give something for nothing is a bare promise (*nudum pactum*). The courts will not enforce bare promises.

#### 3. Practical Example

Engineer P agrees to sell an oscilloscope to Q for Tk. 60,000.
- For P's promise to deliver the instrument, the consideration is Tk. 60,000.
- For Q's promise to pay Tk. 60,000, the consideration is the oscilloscope.

Both parties gain a benefit and undertake an obligation.

### 🎯 GQ2 · Model Exam Question 2 · Three Types of Consideration with Examples (03 Marks)

> **Appeared:** Prescribed Model Question · Chapter 2 Target Part (b) · (03 Marks)

**Question:**
Explain the three types of consideration recognized under the law of contract with suitable examples.

**Full Answer:**

Consideration is classified into three types based on the time of performance:

#### 1. Past Consideration
- **Definition:** The act or service was performed before the promise was made. The promisor later promises compensation for that completed act.
- **Legal Status:** Under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872, past consideration is valid in Bangladesh. The statute includes acts where the promisee "has done or abstained from doing" something.
- **Example:** X repairs Y's laptop in January without asking for payment. In February, Y promises to pay X Tk. 2,500 for that past help. X's service is valid past consideration. Y's promise is legally binding.

#### 2. Present (Executed) Consideration
- **Definition:** Consideration moves simultaneously with the promise. One party fulfills his obligation at the exact time the contract is made.
- **Legal Status:** Valid in all legal systems.
- **Example:** A customer buys microcontrollers at a retail counter and pays cash immediately. The cash payment is present or executed consideration.

#### 3. Future (Executory) Consideration
- **Definition:** Consideration moves at a future date. Both parties exchange mutual promises to perform in the future.
- **Legal Status:** Valid and common in engineering contracts.
- **Example:** Walton agrees to deliver 200 circuit boards next month. An assembly firm promises to pay Tk. 500,000 on delivery. Both obligations remain executory until performed.

### 🎯 GQ3 · Model Exam Question 3 · Essential Legal Rules Governing Valid Consideration (04 Marks)

> **Appeared:** Prescribed Model Question · Chapter 2 Target Part (b) or (c) · (04 Marks)

**Question:**
State and explain the essential legal rules governing valid consideration under the Contract Act, 1872.

**Full Answer:**

Under the Contract Act, 1872, valid consideration must satisfy seven essential legal rules:

#### 1. Must Move at the Desire of the Promisor
The act or forbearance must happen at the request of the promisor. Voluntary acts done without a request create no consideration (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*). Saving goods from a fire without the owner's request does not create a right to payment.

#### 2. May Move from the Promisee or Any Other Person
Under Section 2(d), consideration need not come from the promisee alone. It may move from a third person (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*). A stranger to consideration can enforce a contract in Bangladesh.

#### 3. May Be Past, Present, or Future
Section 2(d) recognizes all three timeframes. It covers acts already done, acts currently performed, and mutual promises for the future.

#### 4. Must Be Real and Not Illusory
Consideration must have value in the eyes of the law. It cannot be physically or legally impossible. A promise to discover treasure by magic is void.

#### 5. Need Not Be Adequate
Under **Section 25, Explanation 2**, an agreement with free consent is valid even if consideration is inadequate. The court does not fix commercial prices. Selling a Tk. 50,000 motor for Tk. 5,000 creates a binding contract if consent is free.

#### 6. Must Be Lawful
Under Section 23, consideration must not be illegal, immoral, or opposed to public policy. An agreement to pay money to forge an academic certificate is void.

#### 7. Must Not Be a Pre-Existing Legal Duty
Doing what a person is already bound to do by law or official duty is not valid consideration. A police officer cannot claim a private cash reward for arresting an offender while on duty.

### 🎯 GQ4 · Model Exam Question 4 · Privity of Contract and Stranger Rules (03 Marks)

> **Appeared:** Prescribed Model Question · Chapter 2 Target Part (b) · (03 Marks)

**Question:**
Explain the doctrine of Privity of Contract. Discuss the statement: "A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue." State the exceptions to this doctrine.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Doctrine of Privity of Contract

Privity of contract means that a contract is a private bargain between the parties who made it. Only parties to a contract can sue to enforce it (*Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co.*). A person who is not a party cannot sue, even if the contract was made for his benefit.

#### 2. Stranger to Consideration vs Stranger to Contract

- **Stranger to consideration:** Under Section 2(d), consideration may move from a third person. A stranger to consideration is a party to the contract. He can sue to enforce it (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*).
- **Stranger to contract:** A stranger to a contract has no contractual relationship with the promisor. He cannot sue.

Therefore, the statement is correct: a stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue.

#### 3. Exceptions Where a Stranger to a Contract Can Sue

A person who is not a party to a contract can sue in the following five cases:
1. **Beneficiaries under a trust:** A trust beneficiary can sue the trustee to enforce his rights under the trust deed.
2. **Family and marriage settlements:** Written family arrangements for maintenance or marriage expenses can be enforced directly by the named relative.
3. **Acknowledgment or estoppel:** The promisor may admit that he holds funds for a third party. That third party can sue to recover the money.
4. **Covenants running with land:** A buyer who acquires land with notice of attached obligations is bound by them. The affected neighbor can sue him directly.
5. **Assignment of rights:** An assignee of a contract or debt can sue the debtor in his own name.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Quote Section 2(d) accurately:** Shoaib Sir checks the exact statutory phrasing. Write: *"When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person..."*
2. **Remember English Law differences:** In English law, past consideration is invalid, and consideration must move from the promisee. In Bangladesh, past consideration is valid, and consideration may move from any person.
3. **Inadequacy does not void a contract:** Many students mistakenly write that selling goods below market value makes a deal void. It does not. Cite Section 25, Explanation 2.
4. **Distinguish the two strangers:** Never mix up a stranger to consideration with a stranger to a contract. Memorize the two cases: *Chinnaya v. Ramayya* and *Dunlop v. Selfridge*.
