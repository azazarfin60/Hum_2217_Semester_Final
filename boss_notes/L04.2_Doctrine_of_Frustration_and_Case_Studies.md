## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Initial Impossibility:** "An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void." (Contract Act, 1872, Section 56, Paragraph 1)

If an act cannot be done from day one, the agreement has no legal effect. The law treats it as void from the very start.

> **Subsequent or Supervening Impossibility:** "A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful." (Contract Act, 1872, Section 56, Paragraph 2)

An agreement starts out valid. Later, an unexpected outside event occurs without fault by either party. This event makes performance physically impossible or illegal. The contract then becomes void.

> **Doctrine of Frustration:** The common law rule found in Section 56. When an unexpected event destroys the basic foundation of a contract, the contract ends automatically.

Both parties are excused from future performance. Neither party can sue for breach damages.

> **Restitution of Advantage:** "When an agreement is discovered to be void, or when a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage under such agreement or contract is bound to restore it, or to make compensation for it to the person from whom he received it." (Contract Act, 1872, Section 65)

If a contract becomes void through frustration, advance payments cannot be kept. The party holding the advance must return it in full.

## Initial vs. Subsequent Impossibility (Section 56)

Section 56 covers two separate kinds of impossibility:

| Comparison Point | Initial Impossibility (`Sec 56 Para 1`) | Subsequent Impossibility (`Sec 56 Para 2`) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Timing** | Exists at the exact moment the agreement is made. | Arises after the contract is formed. |
| **Legal Status** | Void from the very beginning (*void ab initio*). | Valid when formed, but becomes void later. |
| **Cause** | Inherent impossibility in nature or law. | External, unforeseen supervening event. |
| **Example** | An agreement to bring a dead person to life. | A hall burns down accidentally before a scheduled concert. |
| **Liability** | Void agreement; no valid contract ever existed. | Contract ends automatically without breach damages. |

## 5 Valid Grounds of Supervening Impossibility (D-D-L-N-W)

Under Section 56 (Paragraph 2), a contract is discharged only by specific recognized grounds:

1. **Destruction of Subject Matter:**
   The specific subject matter essential to the contract is destroyed without fault of either party.
   - *Leading Case:* *Taylor v. Caldwell* (1863). A music hall was rented for concerts. The hall burned down accidentally before the concert day. The court held the contract discharged.
   - *ECE Example:* An accidental fire destroys a custom telecom transmitter before the agreed delivery date.

2. **Death or Personal Incapacity:**
   Contracts based on personal skill, art, or expertise end if the promisor dies or falls seriously ill.
   - *Leading Case:* *Robinson v. Davison* (1871). A pianist became seriously ill and could not play at a concert. The court held the contract discharged without liability.
   - *ECE Example:* A lead chip architect suffers a severe illness and cannot design custom silicon.

3. **Change of Law or Government Ban:**
   A new law, statute, or government order makes future performance illegal.
   - *Example:* The government imposes an immediate import ban on specific radar equipment. The supplier cannot deliver legally, so the contract becomes void.

4. **Non-Occurrence of Contemplated Event:**
   A contract is entered based on the expected occurrence of a specific event. If that event does not happen, the foundation of the deal fails.
   - *Leading Case:* *Krell v. Henry* (1903). A flat was rented to watch the royal coronation parade. The parade was postponed due to the King's illness. The court held the contract frustrated.
   - *Rule:* The event must be the sole foundation of the contract.

5. **Outbreak of War:**
   War makes performance impossible or turns the other party into an alien enemy.
   - *Rule:* Trading with an alien enemy during wartime is unlawful. Contracts with enemies are dissolved or suspended until peace returns.

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 5 Valid Grounds of Frustration
> Remember **D-D-L-N-W**:
> - **D**: **D**estruction of subject matter (*Taylor v. Caldwell*)
> - **D**: **D**eath or personal incapacity in personal skill contracts
> - **L**: **L**aw change making performance illegal
> - **N**: **N**on-occurrence of foundational event (*Krell v. Henry*)
> - **W**: **W**ar outbreak making trade with an enemy unlawful

## 5 Non-Qualifying Excuses (C-D-S-F-P)

Parties often claim unexpected trouble as an excuse. But Indian and Bangladeshi courts reject these five excuses under Section 56:

1. **Commercial Hardship / Price Rise:**
   Higher market prices, currency drops, or lost profits do not discharge a contract. Performance is still physically and legally possible.
   - *Rule:* Increased cost of performance is an ordinary business risk. It never creates legal frustration.

2. **Default of a Third Party:**
   A seller cannot escape liability because his supplier or subcontractor failed to deliver components.
   - *Rule:* The promisor bears full responsibility for his supply chain.

3. **Strikes, Lock-Outs, and Civil Disturbances:**
   Labor strikes, factory lock-outs, or local transport protests do not discharge a contract.
   - *Rule:* These events pause operations temporarily. They excuse delay only if the contract has a written *force majeure* clause.

4. **Failure of One of Several Objects:**
   A contract may have several purposes. If one purpose fails while others remain possible, the contract survives.
   - *Leading Case:* *Herne Bay Steamboat Co. v. Hutton* (1903). A boat was hired to see a naval review and cruise around the fleet. The review was canceled, but the fleet remained. The contract was not frustrated.

5. **Self-Induced Impossibility:**
   The impossibility arises from a party's own deliberate choice, act, or neglect.
   - *Rule:* Self-induced impossibility is an actionable breach of contract. It can never be claimed as frustration.

> [!tip] Memory Hook: 5 Rejected Excuses
> Remember **C-D-S-F-P**:
> - **C**: **C**ommercial hardship (price surge or lost profit)
> - **D**: **D**efault of third party or subcontractor
> - **S**: **S**trikes, lock-outs, or civil unrest
> - **F**: **F**ailure of one object (other objects still possible)
> - **P**: **P**arty's self-induced impossibility (own act or neglect)

### Valid Frustration Grounds vs. Rejected Excuses

| Valid Frustration Grounds (`D-D-L-N-W`) | Rejected Excuses (`C-D-S-F-P`) |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Destruction:** Subject matter destroyed by accident. | **Commercial Hardship:** Input cost jumped by 300%. |
| **Death / Incapacity:** Personal artist or expert dies. | **Third-Party Default:** Chip supplier missed delivery. |
| **Change of Law:** Government bans the item. | **Strikes:** Factory workers staged a 10-day walkout. |
| **Non-Occurrence:** Foundational event canceled. | **Partial Failure:** One of two planned goals failed. |
| **War:** Promisor becomes an alien enemy. | **Self-Induced:** Promisor neglected to book cargo space. |

## Legal Effects of Frustration and Restitution (Section 65)

When supervening impossibility strikes, four direct legal consequences follow:

1. **Automatic Termination:** The contract ends automatically on the date of the frustrating event. No party needs to serve a notice.
2. **Release from Future Duties:** Both parties are freed from all future promises and obligations.
3. **No Breach Damages:** Neither party committed a wrongful act. So no party can claim damages under Section 73.
4. **Mandatory Restitution (Section 65):**
   - Any advantage or advance payment received under the void contract must be refunded.
   - The recipient cannot keep unearned money. Keeping it creates unjust enrichment.

### 🎯 GQ1 · Model Exam Question · Supervening Impossibility and Doctrine of Frustration (05 Marks)

> **Appeared:** Prescribed Model Exam Question · Target: Part (b), (c), or Applied Case Question · (05 Marks)

**Question:**
Explain the Doctrine of Supervening Impossibility (Doctrine of Frustration) under Section 56 of the Contract Act, 1872. State the valid grounds of frustration and the situations that do not discharge a contract.

**Full Answer:**

#### 1. Meaning and Statutory Rule under Section 56

Under the Contract Act, 1872, impossibility can be initial or subsequent. Initial impossibility makes an agreement void from the start (*void ab initio*).

Subsequent impossibility happens after contract formation. The act was possible when agreed. But an unforeseen event later makes it impossible or unlawful.

Under **Section 56 (Paragraph 2)** of the Contract Act, 1872:
> "A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful."

This statutory rule is known as the **Doctrine of Frustration**. The contract terminates automatically. Under **Section 65**, any party who received an advantage must return it.

#### 2. Five Valid Grounds of Frustration (`D-D-L-N-W`)

1. **Destruction of Subject Matter:** The specific subject matter is destroyed without fault of either party (*Taylor v. Caldwell*).
2. **Death or Personal Incapacity:** In personal service contracts, death or serious illness discharges the contract (*Robinson v. Davison*).
3. **Change of Law:** A new law or government ban makes performance illegal.
4. **Non-Occurrence of Contemplated Event:** The foundational event of the contract fails to occur (*Krell v. Henry*).
5. **Outbreak of War:** War makes performance impossible or turns the other party into an alien enemy.

#### 3. Five Situations That Do NOT Frustrate a Contract (`C-D-S-F-P`)

1. **Commercial Hardship:** Higher prices, wage increases, or reduced profits do not discharge a contract.
2. **Default of a Third Party:** Failure of a chosen supplier or subcontractor does not excuse the promisor.
3. **Strikes and Lock-outs:** Labor disputes or temporary factory shutdowns do not end the contract.
4. **Failure of One Object:** If a contract has multiple purposes and only one fails, the contract survives.
5. **Self-Induced Impossibility:** Impossibility caused by a party's own deliberate act or neglect is a breach, not frustration.

### 🎯 GQ2 · Model Case Study 1 · Tech Solutions Ltd. · Supervening Impossibility vs Commercial Hardship (10 Marks)

> **Appeared:** High-Yield 10-Mark Applied Case Study Target · Shoaib Sir Exam Suggestion · (10 Marks)

#### Case 1 Factual Scenario

*Tech Solutions Ltd.* (an ECE engineering firm in Dhaka) contracts with *PowerGrid Bangladesh Ltd.* It agrees to build and supply 100 custom industrial rectifiers for Tk. 5,000,000 by 15th December 2025. PowerGrid pays an advance of Tk. 1,000,000 upon signing the contract.

On 1st November 2025, a global shortage hit the semiconductor market. The open market price of specialized microcontroller chips jumped by 300%. If Tech Solutions fulfills the contract, it will suffer a financial loss of Tk. 1,500,000. Tech Solutions writes to PowerGrid to cancel the deal due to severe market hardship. PowerGrid refuses to cancel.

On 20th November 2025, a municipal power surge sparked an accidental electrical fire. The fire burned down Tech Solutions' only manufacturing plant without any fault by Tech Solutions. All machinery and 60 partially finished rectifiers were destroyed. Tech Solutions informs PowerGrid that performance is physically impossible and that the contract is terminated.

PowerGrid sues Tech Solutions for breach of contract. It demands Tk. 2,000,000 in damages for project delay. PowerGrid also argues that Tech Solutions used the fire to escape higher chip prices.

#### Case 1 Sub-Questions

- **(i)** Can Tech Solutions Ltd. claim discharge of contract based on the chip price surge? Explain under relevant legal principles. **(03 Marks)**
- **(ii)** Did the accidental factory fire cause Supervening Impossibility under Section 56? What is the legal status of the contract? **(03 Marks)**
- **(iii)** Is PowerGrid entitled to damages for breach of contract? Can PowerGrid recover its Tk. 1,000,000 advance payment? **(04 Marks)**

#### Case 1 Model Solution

##### Solution to Case 1 Sub-Question (i) · Chip Price Surge · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
Tech Solutions faced a 300% price surge for microcontroller chips. Fulfilling the order would cause a loss of Tk. 1,500,000. Tech Solutions sought to cancel the contract on this ground.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 56** (Doctrine of Supervening Impossibility) and the rule on **Commercial Hardship**.

**3. Legal Analysis:**
The law clearly separates physical impossibility from commercial difficulty.
- A contract is not frustrated simply because performance becomes more expensive or unprofitable.
- Microcontroller chips were still available on the open market, although at a higher price.
- Raw material price swings are ordinary business risks.
- Under Section 56, commercial hardship never excuses a party from performing an agreed promise.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
Tech Solutions Ltd. cannot claim discharge based on the chip price surge. The contract remained fully binding on 1st November 2025.

---

##### Solution to Case 1 Sub-Question (ii) · Accidental Factory Fire · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
On 20th November 2025, an accidental fire burned down Tech Solutions' only factory. A municipal power surge caused the fire without fault by Tech Solutions. All work-in-progress rectifiers and machinery were destroyed.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 56 (Paragraph 2)** and the **Doctrine of Frustration** (*Taylor v. Caldwell* rule on destruction of subject matter).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
Under Section 56, an unforeseen event may destroy the essential subject matter without party fault. Performance then becomes physically impossible.
- The rectifiers were custom-designed units. Tech Solutions could only build them in that specific facility before 15th December 2025.
- The fire was caused by an external municipal power surge. Tech Solutions was not negligent.
- The fire destroyed the manufacturing facility and ruined the foundation of performance.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
The accidental fire caused genuine Supervening Impossibility under Section 56. The contract became void automatically on 20th November 2025. Both parties were freed from future performance.

---

##### Solution to Case 1 Sub-Question (iii) · Damages and Advance Recovery · 04 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
PowerGrid sued Tech Solutions for Tk. 2,000,000 in breach damages. PowerGrid also demanded the return of its Tk. 1,000,000 advance payment.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872:
- **Section 56** (Discharge by Frustration)
- **Section 65** (Restitution of advantage received under void contract)
- **Section 73** (Compensation for loss caused by breach)

**3. Legal Analysis:**
- *Claim for Breach Damages (Section 73):* A party can claim damages only after a wrongful breach. Here, the contract was discharged under Section 56 through supervening impossibility. The contract became void without fault by Tech Solutions. Tech Solutions committed no breach. So PowerGrid cannot claim any compensation or damages.
- *Claim for Advance Refund (Section 65):* Under Section 65, when a contract becomes void, any party who received an advantage must return it. Tech Solutions received Tk. 1,000,000 as an advance for rectifiers it can no longer deliver. Tech Solutions cannot keep this unearned money.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
PowerGrid is not entitled to recover Tk. 2,000,000 in breach damages. But PowerGrid is legally entitled to a full refund of its Tk. 1,000,000 advance under Section 65.

### 🎯 GQ3 · Model Case Study 2 · Concert Hall Destruction & Singer Illness (10 Marks)

> **Appeared:** High-Yield 10-Mark Applied Case Study Target · Shoaib Sir Exam Suggestion · (10 Marks)

#### Case 2 Factual Scenario

*Dhaka Cultural Arena Ltd.* contracts with *Harmony Events Ltd.* to lease its central concert auditorium for a three-day national music gala from 20th to 22nd December 2025. Harmony pays a booking deposit of Tk. 300,000 upon signing the contract.

Harmony also enters into a separate personal service contract with *Ustad Farhan*, a celebrated classical vocalist and acoustic master. Ustad Farhan agrees to perform a 3-hour headline opening concert on 20th December for Tk. 200,000. Harmony pays him an advance fee of Tk. 50,000.

On 14th December 2025, an accidental electrical fire breaks out from a municipal short circuit in the neighboring power pole. The fire spreads rapidly and burns the concert hall to the ground without any fault by Dhaka Cultural Arena.

On 18th December 2025, Ustad Farhan contracts acute viral pneumonia and severe vocal cord paralysis. A government medical specialist certifies in writing that singing will permanently damage his vocal cords.

Harmony cancels the entire gala. Harmony sues Dhaka Cultural Arena for Tk. 500,000 in lost ticket profits and demands the return of the Tk. 300,000 deposit. Dhaka Cultural Arena refuses both claims. It argues that the fire was an act of God.

Harmony also sues Ustad Farhan for Tk. 100,000 in wasted marketing costs and demands the return of the Tk. 50,000 advance fee. Ustad Farhan argues that illness was beyond his control and refuses to refund the advance.

#### Case 2 Sub-Questions

- **(i)** Did the accidental destruction of the concert hall discharge the venue lease contract under Section 56? Is Dhaka Cultural Arena liable for Harmony's lost ticket profits? **(03 Marks)**
- **(ii)** What is the legal status of the personal performance contract between Harmony Events and Ustad Farhan? Is Ustad Farhan liable for promotion damages? **(03 Marks)**
- **(iii)** Must Dhaka Cultural Arena refund the Tk. 300,000 venue deposit, and must Ustad Farhan refund the Tk. 50,000 advance fee to Harmony Events? Apply Section 65 restitution rules. **(04 Marks)**

#### Case 2 Model Solution

##### Solution to Case 2 Sub-Question (i) · Concert Hall Fire · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
On 14th December 2025, an accidental municipal fire destroyed the leased concert auditorium. The fire occurred without any fault or negligence by the venue owner.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 56 (Paragraph 2)** (Doctrine of Frustration) and the landmark rule of ***Taylor v. Caldwell* (1863)** (destruction of subject matter).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
Under Section 56, when the specific subject matter essential to a contract is destroyed without fault of either party, performance becomes physically impossible.
- The lease contract depended entirely on the continued physical existence of the central auditorium.
- The fire destroyed the hall completely, removing the foundation of the agreement.
- The event occurred without fault or neglect by Dhaka Cultural Arena Ltd.
- Under Section 56, the contract became void automatically on 14th December 2025.
- Because no wrongful breach took place, Dhaka Cultural Arena is not liable for breach damages under Section 73.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
The accidental fire discharged the venue lease contract under Section 56. Dhaka Cultural Arena Ltd. is not liable for Harmony's Tk. 500,000 lost ticket profits.

---

##### Solution to Case 2 Sub-Question (ii) · Singer Incapacity · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
Ustad Farhan contracted acute pneumonia and vocal cord paralysis. A certified medical doctor confirmed that singing would cause permanent voice loss. Harmony sued him for Tk. 100,000 in marketing costs.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 56 (Paragraph 2)** and the landmark rule of ***Robinson v. Davison* (1871)** (discharge by personal illness or incapacity).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
Under Section 56, contracts for personal service or artistic skill depend on the personal health and capacity of the promisor.
- A vocal performance requires the unique artistic talent and physical health of the vocalist.
- Sudden severe illness making performance dangerous to health excuses the artist from performing.
- Ustad Farhan did not voluntarily cause his pneumonia or vocal cord condition.
- The contract ended automatically due to supervening medical incapacity.
- The failure to sing was an excuse under the law, not a wrongful breach of contract.
- So Harmony cannot claim Tk. 100,000 in promotional damages under Section 73.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
The contract between Harmony Events and Ustad Farhan became void under Section 56 due to personal incapacity. Ustad Farhan is not liable for any marketing or breach damages.

---

##### Solution to Case 2 Sub-Question (iii) · Advance Deposit Restitution · 04 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
Dhaka Cultural Arena held a Tk. 300,000 venue deposit. Ustad Farhan held a Tk. 50,000 performance advance. Both parties refused to return the funds after the contracts became void.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 65** (Obligation of person who has received advantage under void agreement or contract that becomes void).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
- Section 65 establishes the doctrine of restitution. It prevents unjust enrichment when a contract becomes void without fault.
- When an agreement becomes void under Section 56, any party who received an advantage must restore it.
- Dhaka Cultural Arena provided no hall on the festival days. Keeping the Tk. 300,000 deposit would give it unearned profit without consideration.
- Ustad Farhan performed no concert. Keeping the Tk. 50,000 advance fee would give him an unearned benefit.
- Section 65 applies strictly to frustrated contracts. Neither party can keep advance payments for services never delivered.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
Both parties must return the advances. Dhaka Cultural Arena Ltd. must refund the Tk. 300,000 venue deposit in full. Ustad Farhan must refund the Tk. 50,000 advance fee in full to Harmony Events Ltd. under Section 65.

### 🎯 GQ4 · Model Case Study 3 · Government Import Ban vs Strike & Supplier Default (10 Marks)

> **Appeared:** High-Yield 10-Mark Applied Case Study Target · Shoaib Sir Exam Suggestion · (10 Marks)

#### Case 3 Factual Scenario

*Bengal MicroSystems Ltd.* in Dhaka enters a contract with *National Telecom Corporation (NTC)* on 1st October 2025. It agrees to supply two distinct consignments by 31st December 2025:
1. **Consignment A:** 50 encrypted satellite transceivers imported from Germany for Tk. 6,000,000. NTC pays an advance of Tk. 1,200,000 upon signing.
2. **Consignment B:** 2,000 standard fiber patch cables and network switches for Tk. 2,000,000. NTC pays an advance of Tk. 400,000 upon signing.

On 15th November 2025, the Government of Bangladesh gazettes an immediate, emergency statutory ban on importing foreign encrypted satellite equipment for national security reasons. No import permits can be issued.

For Consignment B, Bengal MicroSystems' factory workers launch an unauthorized 20-day strike over yearly festival bonuses. Bengal's chosen local switch supplier also defaults on shipping switches due to factory maintenance delays.

On 31st December 2025, Bengal delivers neither consignment. Bengal claims that both contracts are discharged by Supervening Impossibility under Section 56. It refuses to pay any delay damages and refuses to return the Tk. 1,600,000 total advance.

NTC sues Bengal MicroSystems. It claims Tk. 1,000,000 in delay damages for Consignment A, Tk. 500,000 in delay damages for Consignment B, and a full refund of all advance money.

#### Case 3 Sub-Questions

- **(i)** Did the government import ban discharge the contract for Consignment A under Section 56? Is Bengal liable for Tk. 1,000,000 in delay damages? **(03 Marks)**
- **(ii)** Can Bengal claim discharge of contract for Consignment B based on the factory labor strike and supplier default? **(03 Marks)**
- **(iii)** Is NTC entitled to damages for Consignment B? Must Bengal refund the advance of Tk. 1,200,000 (Consignment A) and Tk. 400,000 (Consignment B) under Section 65? **(04 Marks)**

#### Case 3 Model Solution

##### Solution to Case 3 Sub-Question (i) · Government Import Ban · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
The government gazetted an emergency ban prohibiting the import of satellite transceivers. Bengal could not legally import the units for Consignment A.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 56 (Paragraph 2)** (Supervening illegality caused by a change of law).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
Under Section 56, a contract becomes void if an event makes performance unlawful after the contract was made.
- The government statutory ban made importing the encrypted transceivers illegal in Bangladesh.
- Performance became legally impossible by an act of state authority.
- The change of law occurred without fault or collusion by Bengal MicroSystems.
- Under Section 56, the contract for Consignment A became void automatically on 15th November 2025.
- Because termination was caused by operation of law, Bengal committed no wrongful breach under Section 73.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
The government import ban discharged the contract for Consignment A under Section 56. Bengal MicroSystems Ltd. is not liable for Tk. 1,000,000 in delay damages.

---

##### Solution to Case 3 Sub-Question (ii) · Strike and Supplier Default · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
Bengal failed to deliver Consignment B because factory workers went on strike and its local switch vendor missed shipments. Bengal claimed frustration under Section 56.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 56** and the rules on non-qualifying excuses: **Strikes** and **Third-Party Default**.

**3. Legal Analysis:**
The law strictly distinguishes genuine impossibility from commercial or operational hurdles.
- Strikes and labor disputes do not discharge a contract under Section 56. They represent temporary operational difficulties.
- Default by a third-party supplier does not excuse the promisor. The promisor is legally responsible for managing its supply chain.
- Standard fiber cables and switches were available from other market suppliers in Bangladesh.
- Performance remained both physically and legally possible.
- Bengal failed to perform on the due date. This failure constitutes an actual breach of contract.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
Bengal cannot claim discharge of contract for Consignment B. Labor strikes and supplier defaults are rejected excuses under Section 56. The contract remained fully binding.

---

##### Solution to Case 3 Sub-Question (iii) · Breach Damages and Restitution · 04 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
NTC claimed Tk. 500,000 damages for Consignment B. NTC also demanded the refund of Tk. 1,200,000 for Consignment A and Tk. 400,000 for Consignment B.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872:
- **Section 73** (Compensation for loss or damage caused by breach of contract)
- **Section 65** (Restitution of advantage received under void contract or rescinded agreement)

**3. Legal Analysis:**
- *Consignment B Damages (Section 73):* Bengal breached the contract for Consignment B without a lawful excuse. NTC suffered project delay and loss. Under Section 73, NTC is legally entitled to recover Tk. 500,000 in proven damages.
- *Consignment A Advance Restitution (Section 65):* The contract for Consignment A became void under Section 56. Under Section 65, any person who received an advantage under a void contract must restore it. Bengal received Tk. 1,200,000 as an advance for gear it cannot deliver. Bengal must refund Tk. 1,200,000 in full.
- *Consignment B Advance Restitution (Section 65 / Breach):* Bengal delivered no cables or switches. It cannot retain the Tk. 400,000 advance for goods it failed to provide. Bengal must return the Tk. 400,000 advance.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
NTC is legally entitled to recover Tk. 500,000 in breach damages for Consignment B. In addition, Bengal must refund the Tk. 1,200,000 advance for Consignment A and the Tk. 400,000 advance for Consignment B under Section 65.

### 🎯 GQ5 · Model Case Study 4 · Anticipatory Breach and Novation in Telecom Project (10 Marks)

> **Appeared:** High-Yield 10-Mark Applied Case Study Target · Shoaib Sir Exam Suggestion · (10 Marks)

#### Case 4 Factual Scenario

*FiberNet Ltd.* (a telecom provider) contracts with *TeleSoft Solutions Ltd.* on 1st January 2025. TeleSoft agrees to build a fiber billing platform for Tk. 3,000,000 by 30th June 2025. FiberNet pays an advance of Tk. 600,000 upon signing.

On 1st April 2025, TeleSoft sent an official letter to FiberNet: *"Our key software architects have left. We cannot deliver the software on 30th June 2025. We repudiate the agreement and withdraw from the project."*

FiberNet did not sue right away. Instead, FiberNet called a meeting on 15th April 2025 with TeleSoft and *CloudCore Systems Ltd.*

At this meeting, all three parties signed a written tripartite agreement:
1. TeleSoft is fully released from all future obligations under the original contract.
2. CloudCore agrees to take over the codebase and complete the billing platform by 31st August 2025 for Tk. 2,400,000.
3. FiberNet accepts CloudCore in place of TeleSoft.

Two weeks later, FiberNet demanded the return of its Tk. 600,000 advance. TeleSoft refused to pay. It argued that the old contract was discharged. It also claimed that FiberNet lost all refund rights by signing the new deal.

#### Case 4 Sub-Questions

- **(i)** What was the legal effect of TeleSoft's letter on 1st April 2025? What legal options did FiberNet hold under Section 39? **(03 Marks)**
- **(ii)** Evaluate the tripartite agreement signed on 15th April 2025 under Section 62. Is TeleSoft discharged from its original contractual liabilities? **(03 Marks)**
- **(iii)** Must TeleSoft return the Tk. 600,000 advance payment to FiberNet? Justify your decision under the principles of Novation and Restitution. **(04 Marks)**

#### Case 4 Model Solution

##### Solution to Case 4 Sub-Question (i) · Anticipatory Breach · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
On 1st April 2025, TeleSoft sent a letter withdrawing from the project. This notice came three months before the agreed due date of 30th June 2025.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 39** (Anticipatory Breach of Contract).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
Anticipatory breach occurs when a party renounces his liability before performance is due. TeleSoft clearly stated that it would not perform.
Under **Section 39**, FiberNet had two lawful courses of action:
1. *Accept the repudiation immediately:* Treat the contract as ended on 1st April 2025. Sue at once for breach and damages.
2. *Keep the contract alive:* Refuse the repudiation and hold TeleSoft liable. Then wait for actual performance on 30th June 2025.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
TeleSoft's letter was an Anticipatory Breach of contract. FiberNet held two legal choices. It could end the contract immediately and sue for damages, or keep the contract alive until the due date.

---

##### Solution to Case 4 Sub-Question (ii) · Discharge by Novation · 03 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
FiberNet, TeleSoft, and CloudCore signed a written agreement. This agreement substituted CloudCore for TeleSoft and released TeleSoft from future duties.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 62** (Discharge by Novation).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
Novation means replacing an existing contract with a new contract. This substitution can happen between the same parties or bring in a new party.
- For a valid novation, all three parties (the creditor, the original debtor, and the new debtor) must give mutual consent.
- FiberNet, TeleSoft, and CloudCore all signed the agreement willingly.
- CloudCore promised to complete the work, and FiberNet accepted CloudCore. This formed the consideration for releasing TeleSoft.
- Under Section 62, a valid novation extinguishes the original contract completely.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
The tripartite agreement is a valid Novation under Section 62. TeleSoft is legally discharged from all future duties under the original contract.

---

##### Solution to Case 4 Sub-Question (iii) · Advance Restitution · 04 Marks

**1. Facts of the Case:**
TeleSoft refused to return the Tk. 600,000 advance after novation. It claimed the original contract was discharged without an express refund clause.

**2. Applicable Law & Section:**
Contract Act, 1872, **Section 62** (Novation rules) and **Section 65** (Restitution of advantage under void or ended contract).

**3. Legal Analysis:**
- Under novation, the new agreement replaces the rights and duties of the old contract.
- The original contract price was Tk. 3,000,000. CloudCore agreed to complete the project for Tk. 2,400,000.
- This left the exact Tk. 600,000 balance that FiberNet had already paid.
- TeleSoft delivered no working software. If it keeps the advance, it enjoys unjust enrichment without providing consideration.
- Under **Section 65**, a party must return any advantage received under a contract that becomes void or ends. This rule applies unless the parties agreed in writing to forfeit the advance.
- Novation freed TeleSoft from future work. But it gave TeleSoft no right to keep unearned advance funds.

**4. Direct Conclusion:**
TeleSoft must return the Tk. 600,000 advance payment to FiberNet. Keeping the advance without delivering value violates Section 65 restitution rules.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **State Section Numbers Upfront:** Always cite the Contract Act, 1872, Section 56 (Frustration), Section 65 (Restitution), Section 39 (Anticipatory Breach), or Section 62 (Novation). Shoaib Sir deducts marks if sections are missing.
2. **Never Confuse Commercial Hardship with Frustration:** A 300% price hike, labor strike, or third-party vendor default is never frustration. Performance remains physically and legally possible.
3. **Remember the Landmark Doctrines:** Accidental fire destroying the venue discharges the contract (*Taylor v. Caldwell*). Serious personal illness discharges an artist's personal performance contract (*Robinson v. Davison*). A government statutory embargo discharges the contract through supervening illegality.
4. **Remember the Restitution Rule (Section 65):** When a contract becomes void under Section 56, breach damages under Section 73 cannot be claimed. But any advance payment must always be refunded under Section 65 to prevent unjust enrichment.
5. **Use the 4-Step Framework for Case Studies:** Always use the 4 headings: `1. Facts of the Case`, `2. Applicable Law & Section`, `3. Legal Analysis`, and `4. Direct Conclusion`. This structure secures full marks.
