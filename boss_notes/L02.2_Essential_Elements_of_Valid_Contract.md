## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Section 10 (What agreements are contracts):** "All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void." (Contract Act, 1872, Section 10)

Section 10 serves as the legal filter of the Contract Act. It takes ordinary promises and turns them into legally binding contracts.

> **Contract [Section 2(h)]:** "An agreement enforceable by law is a contract." (Contract Act, 1872)

A contract requires two building blocks: an agreement and legal enforceability by courts.

$$
\text{Contract} = \text{Agreement} + \text{Legal Enforceability}
$$

---

## Core Legal Maxim: Contracts vs. Agreements

The foundational maxim of contract law states:

> **"All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts."**

1. **Why all contracts are agreements:**  
   Every contract begins life as an agreement. Under Section 2(e), an agreement forms when parties exchange promises. Without an agreement, no contract can ever exist.

2. **Why all agreements are not contracts:**  
   An agreement becomes a contract only when it satisfies all conditions of Section 10. Social invites, domestic promises, and illegal deals lack legal enforceability. They remain mere agreements.

---

## The 10 Essential Elements of a Valid Contract

To create a valid contract, an agreement must fulfill ten distinct statutory conditions.

### 1. Lawful Offer and Acceptance [Sections 2(a), 2(b), and 7]
Every contract requires at least two parties. One party makes a lawful proposal. The other party accepts it.

Under Section 7(1), acceptance must be absolute and unqualified. Both parties must agree to the exact same terms. This meeting of minds is called *consensus ad idem*.

### 2. Intention to Create Legal Relations
The parties must intend to create a binding legal obligation. If one party breaks the promise, the other party must have the right to go to court.

- **Business Agreements:** The law presumes that commercial deals have legal intent.
- **Social or Domestic Agreements:** The law presumes that social promises lack legal intent. For example, a dinner invitation creates no legal duty. In *Balfour v. Balfour (1919)*, a husband promised to pay his wife a monthly allowance. The court held that domestic promises do not create legal contracts.

### 3. Lawful Consideration [Sections 2(d) and 25]
Consideration means something in return (*quid pro quo*). Each party must give something of value and receive something of value.

- Under Section 25, an agreement made without consideration is void.
- Consideration may be an act, a forbearance, or a promise.
- Consideration must be lawful under Section 23.

### 4. Capacity of Parties [Sections 11 and 12]
The parties must be legally competent to enter into a contract. Under Section 11, a person is competent to contract if they meet three tests:
1. They have reached the age of majority (18 years under the Majority Act, 1875).
2. They are of sound mind under Section 12.
3. They are not disqualified from contracting by any law (such as alien enemies or convicted persons).

An agreement made with a minor is void from the start (*void ab initio*), as established in *Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903)*.

### 5. Free Consent [Sections 13 to 22]
Parties must enter into the contract by free choice. Under Section 13, two or more persons consent when they agree on the same thing in the same sense.

Under Section 14, consent is not free when caused by:
1. **Coercion [Section 15]:** Committing or threatening to commit an act forbidden by the Penal Code, or unlawful detaining of property.
2. **Undue Influence [Section 16]:** Using a position of power or trust to dominate the will of the other party.
3. **Fraud [Section 17]:** Making a false statement with intent to deceive the other party.
4. **Misrepresentation [Section 18]:** Making an innocent false statement without intent to deceive.
5. **Mistake [Sections 20, 21, and 22]:** An erroneous belief about the facts or the law.

If consent is flawed by coercion, fraud, or misrepresentation, Section 19 makes the contract voidable. The injured party can choose to cancel or enforce it. A bilateral mistake of fact makes the agreement void under Section 20.

### 6. Lawful Object [Section 23]
The purpose of the agreement must be lawful. Under Section 23, an agreement is void if its object:
- Is forbidden by law.
- Would defeat the provisions of any law.
- Is fraudulent.
- Involves injury to the person or property of another.
- Is regarded by the court as immoral or opposed to public policy.

For example, an agreement to steal intellectual property or smuggle electronic parts is void.

### 7. Certainty of Terms [Section 29]
The terms of the agreement must be definite, clear, and unambiguous.

Under Section 29, agreements whose meaning is not certain, or cannot be made certain, are void. For example, agreeing to buy "some microcontrollers for a good price" is void for uncertainty.

### 8. Possibility of Performance [Section 56]
The agreement must be physically and legally possible to carry out.

Under Section 56(1), an agreement to do an impossible act is void from the start. For example, a promise to discover treasure by magic is void.

### 9. Agreements Not Expressly Declared Void [Sections 26 to 30]
The agreement must not fall into any category that the Contract Act explicitly bans. The Act expressly declares five types of agreements void:
1. **Restraint of Marriage [Section 26]:** Any agreement that restrains the marriage of an adult is void.
2. **Restraint of Trade [Section 27]:** Every agreement that restrains anyone from exercising a lawful profession, trade, or business is void.
3. **Restraint of Legal Proceedings [Section 28]:** Agreements that restrict a party from enforcing their legal rights in court are void.
4. **Uncertain Agreements [Section 29]:** Agreements with vague or indefinite terms are void.
5. **Wagering Agreements [Section 30]:** Agreements based on betting or wagering are void and cannot be sued upon.

### 10. Legal Formalities (Writing, Registration, and Stamps)
Under the Contract Act, an oral contract is valid in general.

However, specific statutes require written, stamped, or registered documents:
- Sale or mortgage of real estate under the Transfer of Property Act, 1882.
- Compulsory registration of deeds under Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908.
- Corporate contracts under the Companies Act, 1994.

Where the law requires writing or registration, the parties must follow those legal formalities. Otherwise, the contract remains unenforceable in court.

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive & Model Answers)

### 🎯 GQ1 · 2023 Final Q.3(b) · Conditions for Legal Enforceability of a Contract (04 Marks)

> **Appeared:** 2023 Final Q.3(b) · Section A · CO1 · (04 Marks)  
> *(Note: The original question paper printed "contact" instead of "contract".)*

**Question:**  
Summarizes the conditions that must be satisfied for a contact to be legally enforceable.

**Full Answer:**

Under **Section 10** of the **Contract Act, 1872**, all agreements are contracts if they meet specific statutory conditions. For a contract to be legally enforceable, the following elements must be satisfied:

1. **Lawful Offer and Acceptance [Sections 2(a), 2(b), 7]:**  
   One party makes a lawful proposal. The other party accepts it unconditionally. Both parties agree on the same terms.

2. **Intention to Create Legal Relations:**  
   The parties must intend to create a binding legal obligation. Social and domestic promises create no legal obligations.

3. **Lawful Consideration [Sections 2(d) and 25]:**  
   Each party must give and receive value (*quid pro quo*). Under Section 25, an agreement without consideration is void.

4. **Capacity of Parties [Sections 11 and 12]:**  
   Parties must be competent to contract. They must be at least 18 years old, of sound mind, and not disqualified by law.

5. **Free Consent [Sections 13 and 14]:**  
   Consent must be genuine and free. It must not stem from coercion (Section 15) or undue influence (Section 16). It must also be free from fraud (Section 17), misrepresentation (Section 18), or mutual mistake (Section 20).

6. **Lawful Object [Section 23]:**  
   The purpose of the contract must not be illegal, fraudulent, injurious to others, or opposed to public policy.

7. **Certainty and Possibility [Sections 29 and 56]:**  
   Terms must be clear and unambiguous. The act must also be physically and legally possible to perform.

8. **Not Expressly Declared Void [Sections 26 to 30]:**  
   The agreement must not fall into statutory void classes, such as restraint of trade (Section 27) or wagering (Section 30).

### 🎯 GQ2 · 2025 Law-CT1 Q.1 · Role of Essential Elements in Contract Enforceability (10 Marks)

> **Appeared:** 2025 Law-CT1 Q.1 · ECE Department, RUET · CO1 · (10 Marks)

**Question:**  
Explain how the essential elements of a valid contract ensure the enforceability of agreements under the Contract Act.

**Full Answer:**

Under the **Contract Act, 1872**, contract formation begins with an agreement. Section 2(e) defines an agreement as:  
*"Every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other."*

Section 2(h) defines a contract as:  
*"An agreement enforceable by law."*

This relationship gives rise to the classic legal maxim:  
**"All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts."**

An agreement gains legal enforceability only when it satisfies all requirements set out in **Section 10**:

$$
\text{Valid Contract} = \text{Agreement} + \text{Legal Enforceability (Section 10 Essentials)}
$$

The essential elements ensure legal enforceability in the following ways:

1. **Offer and Acceptance [Sections 2(a), 2(b), and 7]:**  
   Enforceability requires mutual assent (*consensus ad idem*). One party makes a proposal, and the other signifies acceptance. Under Section 7(1), acceptance must be absolute and unqualified. Both parties bind themselves to identical terms.

2. **Intention to Create Legal Relations:**  
   The law enforces only promises made with legal intent. In commercial transactions, courts presume this legal intent exists. In social and domestic agreements, courts presume it is absent (*Balfour v. Balfour*). This filter prevents courts from being flooded with casual promises.

3. **Lawful Consideration [Sections 2(d) and 25]:**  
   Consideration is the price paid for a promise (*quid pro quo*). Under Section 25, an agreement without consideration is void. Courts will not enforce one-sided gratuitous promises. Also, the consideration must be lawful under Section 23.

4. **Capacity of Parties [Sections 11 and 12]:**  
   Enforceability requires competent parties. Under Section 11, a contracting party must be at least 18 years old. They must be of sound mind and free from legal disqualification. An agreement with a minor is void from the start (*Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose*). This rule shields young individuals from exploitation.

5. **Free Consent [Sections 13, 14, and 19]:**  
   Parties must enter into the agreement freely. Under Section 14, consent is not free when caused by:
   - Coercion (Section 15)
   - Undue influence (Section 16)
   - Fraud (Section 17)
   - Misrepresentation (Section 18)
   - Bilateral mistake of fact (Section 20)  
   If coercion, fraud, or misrepresentation occurs, Section 19 makes the contract voidable. The injured party can choose to cancel or affirm it. This rule preserves fairness in dealings.

6. **Lawful Object [Section 23]:**  
   The object of the contract must be lawful. Under Section 23, an agreement is void if its purpose breaks the law. It cannot cause injury to others or oppose public policy. Courts will never enforce illegal schemes.

7. **Agreements Not Expressly Declared Void [Sections 26 to 30]:**  
   The Act expressly bans five types of agreements to protect public freedom:
   - Restraint of marriage (Section 26)
   - Restraint of trade or profession (Section 27)
   - Restraint of legal proceedings (Section 28)
   - Uncertain agreements (Section 29)
   - Wagering agreements (Section 30)  
   Barring these deals protects open markets and personal liberties.

8. **Certainty of Terms [Section 29]:**  
   Terms must be precise and definite. Under Section 29, vague agreements are void. A court cannot enforce promises that are not clear.

9. **Possibility of Performance [Section 56]:**  
   Under Section 56(1), an agreement to do an impossible act is void *ab initio*. The law never forces parties to perform impossible acts.

10. **Legal Formalities (Writing and Registration):**  
    While oral contracts are generally valid, certain statutes mandate written and registered instruments. Examples include transfers of immovable property under the Registration Act, 1908. Written formalities provide clear evidence in court.

**Conclusion:**  
These essential elements act as legal filters. They eliminate casual promises, protect vulnerable parties, and prohibit illegal conduct. When all ten elements are present, the agreement becomes an enforceable contract.

### 🎯 GQ3 · Model Exam Question · Essential Elements of a Valid Contract (04 Marks)

> **Appeared:** Prescribed Model Question · Section 10 Checklist · (04 Marks)  
> *(Exam Note: In the 2024 Final paper, Q.2(a) tested contract definitions [02m, answered in L02.1], while Q.2(b) tested employee motivation [03m, answered in M05.1]. In 2023 Final, Q.3(b) tested contract enforceability [04m, shown in GQ1 above]. This 4-mark model question provides the exact, high-scoring summary format for Section 10.)*

**Question:**  
State the essential elements of a valid contract as laid down in Section 10 of the Contract Act, 1872.

**Full Answer:**

Under **Section 10** of the **Contract Act, 1872**, all agreements are contracts if they satisfy these essential conditions:

1. **Lawful Offer and Acceptance [Sections 2(a), 2(b), and 7]:**  
   One party makes a lawful proposal. The other party accepts it without any condition. Both parties agree on the same terms (*consensus ad idem*).

2. **Intention to Create Legal Relations:**  
   The parties must intend to create a binding legal duty. Business agreements carry this intent. Social and domestic promises do not (*Balfour v. Balfour*).

3. **Lawful Consideration [Sections 2(d) and 25]:**  
   Each party must give and receive value (*quid pro quo*). Under Section 25, an agreement without consideration is void.

4. **Capacity of Parties [Sections 11 and 12]:**  
   Each party must be at least 18 years old, of sound mind, and not disqualified by law. Agreements with minors are void from the start (*Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose*).

5. **Free Consent [Sections 13, 14, and 15–22]:**  
   Consent must be voluntary. It must be free from coercion (Sec 15) and undue influence (Sec 16). It must also be free from fraud (Sec 17), misrepresentation (Sec 18), and mutual mistake (Sec 20).

6. **Lawful Object [Section 23]:**  
   The purpose of the agreement must not break the law, harm persons or property, or oppose public policy.

7. **Certainty and Possibility of Performance [Sections 29 and 56]:**  
   Terms must be clear and definite. The act must also be possible to carry out physically and legally.

8. **Not Expressly Declared Void [Sections 26 to 30]:**  
   The agreement must not be banned by the Act, such as restraint of trade (Sec 27) or wagering (Sec 30).

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Always Quote Section 10 First:** When asked about essential elements, start with Section 10 of the Contract Act, 1872. Shoaib Sir looks for this exact opening.
2. **List the 5 Consent Vices with Sections:** Do not just write "consent must be free". List Coercion (Sec 15), Undue Influence (Sec 16), Fraud (Sec 17), Misrepresentation (Sec 18), and Mistake (Sec 20–22).
3. **List the 5 Expressly Void Agreements:** Quote Sections 26 to 30. Mentioning marriage, trade, legal proceedings, uncertainty, and wager scores full marks.
4. **State the Void vs. Voidable Distinction:** Flawed consent from coercion or fraud makes a contract voidable under Section 19. In contrast, agreements with minors are void from the start under Section 11.
5. **Cite the Case Laws:** Add *Balfour v. Balfour* for legal intention and *Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose* for minor capacity.
